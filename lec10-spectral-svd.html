10.1  Symmetric matrices, the spectral theorem, and quadratic forms

💡 Intuition

Lecture 8 met two obstacles to diagonalizing a matrix: eigenvalues that are not real, and eigenspaces that are too small. For a symmetric matrix neither happens, and more is true: the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, so that A=QDQTA = QDQ^{T} with QQ an orthogonal matrix. This is the spectral theorem. Geometrically, a symmetric matrix stretches space along nn mutually perpendicular directions, by the factors λ1,…,λn\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n. The same directions answer an optimization question: on the unit sphere, the quadratic form 𝐱TA𝐱\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x} is largest in the direction of an eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue and smallest in the direction of one for the smallest. In Section 10.2 this is applied to the symmetric matrix ATAA^{T}A of an arbitrary matrix AA, which produces the singular value decomposition.

Definition 10.1 (Symmetric matrix)¶

A square matrix AA is symmetric if AT=AA^{T} = A, that is, aij=ajia_{ij} = a_{ji} for all i,ji, j: the entries are mirrored across the diagonal.

Example 10.2 (Where symmetric matrices come from)¶
  1. For every m×nm \times n matrix AA, the matrices ATAA^{T}A (n×nn \times n) and AATAA^{T} (m×mm \times m) are symmetric: by the transpose rule (BC)T=CTBT(BC)^{T} = C^{T}B^{T} (Theorem 4.17), (ATA)T=AT(AT)T=ATA(A^{T}A)^{T} = A^{T}(A^{T})^{T} = A^{T}A, and likewise for AATAA^{T}. By Lemma 9.15, the entries of ATAA^{T}A are the inner products of the columns of AA.
  2. The matrix of an orthogonal projection is symmetric (part (f) of Theorem 9.34).
  3. The matrices (5222)\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} and (211121112)\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} are symmetric; the rotation (01−10)\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} of Example 8.26, which has no real eigenvalue, is not.
Lemma 10.3 (Symmetric matrices move across the inner product)¶

An n×nn \times n matrix AA is symmetric if and only if (A𝐱)⋅𝐲=𝐱⋅(A𝐲)for all 𝐱,𝐲∈ℝn.(A\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} \cdot (A\mathbf{y}) \qquad\text{for all } \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n .

By the transpose rule, (A𝐱)⋅𝐲=(A𝐱)T𝐲=𝐱TAT𝐲(A\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{y} = (A\mathbf{x})^{T}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}^{T}A^{T}\mathbf{y} and 𝐱⋅(A𝐲)=𝐱TA𝐲\mathbf{x} \cdot (A\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{y}. If AT=AA^{T} = A the two agree. Conversely, taking 𝐱=𝐞i\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_i and 𝐲=𝐞j\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{e}_j gives 𝐞iTAT𝐞j=aji\mathbf{e}_i^{T}A^{T}\mathbf{e}_j = a_{ji} and 𝐞iTA𝐞j=aij\mathbf{e}_i^{T}A\mathbf{e}_j = a_{ij}, so equality for all 𝐱,𝐲\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} forces aij=ajia_{ij} = a_{ji}.

Proposition 10.4 (Eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal)¶

Let AA be symmetric, and let A𝐱=λ𝐱A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} and A𝐲=μ𝐲A\mathbf{y} = \mu\mathbf{y} with λ≠μ\lambda \neq \mu. Then 𝐱⋅𝐲=0\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} = 0.

By Lemma 10.3, λ(𝐱⋅𝐲)=(A𝐱)⋅𝐲=𝐱⋅(A𝐲)=μ(𝐱⋅𝐲)\lambda\,(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}) = (A\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} \cdot (A\mathbf{y}) = \mu\,(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}). So (λ−μ)(𝐱⋅𝐲)=0(\lambda - \mu)(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}) = 0, and since λ−μ≠0\lambda - \mu \neq 0, 𝐱⋅𝐲=0\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} = 0.

Lemma 10.5 (A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues)¶

Every root of the characteristic polynomial of a symmetric matrix AA is a real number. In particular, AA has at least one (real) eigenvalue, and its characteristic polynomial is a product of real linear factors.

We use complex numbers briefly, as in Remark 8.27. For a complex number z=a+biz = a + bi (a,ba, b real) its conjugate is z=a−bi\overline{z} = a - bi. Then zz=(a−bi)(a+bi)=a2+abi−abi−b2i2=a2+b2≥0,\overline{z}\,z = (a - bi)(a + bi) = a^2 + abi - abi - b^2i^2 = a^2 + b^2 \ge 0 , since i2=−1i^2 = -1. Equality holds only for z=0z = 0, and zz is real exactly when z=z\overline{z} = z, that is, when b=0b = 0. Conjugation respects sums and products, and conjugating twice gives back zz: for complex numbers zz and ww, z+w=z+w,zw=zw,z=z.\overline{z + w} = \overline{z} + \overline{w}, \qquad \overline{zw} = \overline{z}\,\overline{w}, \qquad \overline{\overline{z}} = z . For 𝐱=(x1,…,xn)T∈ℂn\mathbf{x} = (x_1,\, \dots,\, x_n)^{T} \in \mathbb{C}^n we write 𝐱=(x1,…,xn)T\overline{\mathbf{x}} = (\overline{x}_1,\, \dots,\, \overline{x}_n)^{T}.

Let λ∈ℂ\lambda \in \mathbb{C} be a root of pAp_A. Then det(A−λIn)=0\det(A - \lambda I_n) = 0, so, by row reduction over ℂ\mathbb{C} exactly as over ℝ\mathbb{R}, there is a nonzero 𝐱∈ℂn\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{C}^n with A𝐱=λ𝐱A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}. Since AA is real, we have A𝐱=A𝐱=λ𝐱=λ𝐱.A\overline{\mathbf{x}} = \overline{A\mathbf{x}} = \overline{\lambda\mathbf{x}} = \overline{\lambda}\,\overline{\mathbf{x}} .

We compute 𝐱TA𝐱\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A\mathbf{x} in two ways. From A𝐱=λ𝐱A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} we get 𝐱TA𝐱=λ𝐱T𝐱\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{x}. On the other hand, by the transpose rule, AT=AA^{T} = A and A𝐱=λ𝐱A\overline{\mathbf{x}} = \overline{\lambda}\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}, 𝐱TA𝐱=(AT𝐱)T𝐱=(A𝐱)T𝐱=(λ𝐱)T𝐱=λ𝐱T𝐱.\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A\mathbf{x} = \left(A^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{T}\mathbf{x} = \left(A\overline{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{T}\mathbf{x} = \left(\overline{\lambda}\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{T}\mathbf{x} = \overline{\lambda}\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{x} . Hence (λ−λ)𝐱T𝐱=0(\lambda - \overline{\lambda})\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{x} = 0.

Now 𝐱T𝐱=x1x1+⋯+xnxn\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{x} = \overline{x}_1x_1 + \cdots + \overline{x}_nx_n, where each xixi≥0\overline{x}_ix_i \ge 0, with xixi>0\overline{x}_ix_i > 0 whenever xi≠0x_i \neq 0; since 𝐱≠0\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}, the sum is positive. Therefore λ=λ\overline{\lambda} = \lambda: the root λ\lambda is real.

By the fundamental theorem of algebra pAp_A splits into linear factors over ℂ\mathbb{C} (Remark 8.27), and we have just seen that all the roots are real, so pAp_A is a product of real linear factors. Finally, for a real root λ\lambda the real matrix A−λInA - \lambda I_n is singular, so it has a nonzero real null vector, which is a real eigenvector (Theorem 8.16).

Definition 10.6 (Orthogonally diagonalizable)¶

A square matrix AA is orthogonally diagonalizable if A=QDQTA = QDQ^{T} for an orthogonal matrix QQ and a diagonal matrix DD; such a factorization is called an orthogonal decomposition of AA. Since QT=Q−1Q^{T} = Q^{-1} for an orthogonal matrix, this is a diagonalization A=PDP−1A = PDP^{-1} as in Theorem 8.30 in which the columns of P=QP = Q are orthonormal: they form an orthonormal basis of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n consisting of eigenvectors of AA.

Remark 10.7 (Block matrices)¶

The proof of the spectral theorem multiplies matrices that are cut into blocks. If an n×nn \times n matrix is written as (a𝐛T𝐜M)\begin{pmatrix} a & \mathbf{b}^{T} \\ \mathbf{c} & M \end{pmatrix}, with a number aa, vectors 𝐛,𝐜∈ℝn−1\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{n-1} and an (n−1)×(n−1)(n-1) \times (n-1) matrix MM, then products are computed block by block, exactly as for 2×22 \times 2 matrices, as long as the sizes match; for instance, (10T0N)(a𝐛T𝐜M)(10T0N′)=(a𝐛TN𝐜NM)(10T0N′)=(a𝐛TN′N𝐜NMN′):\begin{aligned} &\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0}^{T} \\ \mathbf{0} & N \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & \mathbf{b}^{T} \\ \mathbf{c} & M \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0}^{T} \\ \mathbf{0} & N' \end{pmatrix} \\ &\qquad = \begin{pmatrix} a & \mathbf{b}^{T} \\ N\mathbf{c} & NM \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0}^{T} \\ \mathbf{0} & N' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & \mathbf{b}^{T}N' \\ N\mathbf{c} & NMN' \end{pmatrix}: \end{aligned} first the left two factors are multiplied, then the result is multiplied by the factor on the right. This follows from the row–column description of the product (Proposition 4.12): each entry of a product is a sum over one index, and grouping that sum into the first index and the remaining ones gives the block formula.

Theorem 10.8 (The spectral theorem)¶

Remark 10.10 (How to diagonalize a symmetric matrix orthogonally)¶

To write a symmetric matrix AA as QDQTQDQ^{T}:

  1. Find the eigenvalues; by Lemma 10.5 all roots of pAp_A are real.
  2. For each eigenvalue find a basis of its eigenspace by row reduction.
  3. Apply Gram–Schmidt within each eigenspace and normalize. Eigenvectors from different eigenspaces are already orthogonal (Proposition 10.4), so only a repeated eigenvalue needs this step.
  4. Put the vectors into QQ and the eigenvalues, in the same order, into DD; check AQ=QDAQ = QD.

Definition 10.16 (Definite and indefinite forms)¶

A symmetric matrix AA, and its quadratic form, are positive definite if 𝐱TA𝐱>0\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x} > 0 for every 𝐱≠0\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}; positive semidefinite if 𝐱TA𝐱≥0\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x} \ge 0 for every 𝐱\mathbf{x}; negative definite or semidefinite with the inequalities reversed; and indefinite if 𝐱TA𝐱\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x} takes both positive and negative values.

Proposition 10.17 (Definiteness from the eigenvalues)¶

A symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive, positive semidefinite if and only if all are ≥0\ge 0, and indefinite if and only if it has a positive and a negative eigenvalue.

By (10.3), 𝐱TA𝐱=∑iλiyi2\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x} = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2, and 𝐱≠0\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0} exactly when 𝐲≠0\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{0}. Conversely, taking 𝐱=𝐪i\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{q}_i (so 𝐲=𝐞i\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{e}_i) gives 𝐪iTA𝐪i=λi\mathbf{q}_i^{T}A\mathbf{q}_i = \lambda_i, so a positive definite form has every λi>0\lambda_i > 0. The other cases are the same argument.

10.2  The singular value decomposition (SVD)

💡 Intuition

Most matrices are not symmetric, not even square. Still, every m×nm \times n matrix AA maps some orthonormal basis 𝐯1,…,𝐯n\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n to mutually orthogonal vectors A𝐯1,…,A𝐯nA\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, A\mathbf{v}_n of ℝm\mathbb{R}^m. To find them, note that ∥A𝐱∥2=𝐱T(ATA)𝐱\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \mathbf{x}^{T}(A^{T}A)\mathbf{x} is a quadratic form with the symmetric matrix ATAA^{T}A, and use the spectral theorem. Writing A𝐯i=σi𝐮iA\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i with unit vectors 𝐮i\mathbf{u}_i gives A=UΣVTA = U\Sigma V^{T}: a rotation (or reflection) VTV^{T}, then a stretch along the coordinate axes by the singular values σi\sigma_i, then another rotation UU. The figure in this section shows the picture in the plane: the unit circle goes to an ellipse with semi-axes σ1𝐮1\sigma_1\mathbf{u}_1 and σ2𝐮2\sigma_2\mathbf{u}_2.

Lemma 10.22 (The matrix ATAA^{T}A)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n. Then ATAA^{T}A is symmetric, and all of its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative.

ATAA^{T}A is symmetric by Example 10.2, so its eigenvalues are real by Lemma 10.5. Let λ\lambda be an eigenvalue, with a real eigenvector 𝐯\mathbf{v}, so ATA𝐯=λ𝐯A^{T}A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}, normalized so that ∥𝐯∥=1\|\mathbf{v}\| = 1. Then λ=λ𝐯⋅𝐯=𝐯T(ATA𝐯)=(A𝐯)T(A𝐯)=∥A𝐯∥2≥0.\lambda = \lambda\,\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^{T}(A^{T}A\mathbf{v}) = (A\mathbf{v})^{T}(A\mathbf{v}) = \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 \ge 0 .

Definition 10.23 (Singular values)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n, and let λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λn≥0\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0 be the eigenvalues of ATAA^{T}A. The singular values of AA are σi=λi\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}, i=1,…,ni = 1, \dots, n, listed in decreasing order σ1≥σ2≥⋯≥σn≥0\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0 (the λi\lambda_i are real and nonnegative by Lemma 10.22). If 𝐯i\mathbf{v}_i is a unit eigenvector of ATAA^{T}A for λi\lambda_i, then σi=∥A𝐯i∥\sigma_i = \|A\mathbf{v}_i\|, as in the proof of Lemma 10.22: the singular values are the lengths of the images of these eigenvectors. Exactly r=rankAr = \operatorname{rank} A of them are positive. Indeed, by the spectral theorem ATA=VDVTA^{T}A = VDV^{T} with D=diag(λ1,…,λn)D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n), so rank(ATA)=rankD\operatorname{rank}(A^{T}A) = \operatorname{rank} D is the number of nonzero λi\lambda_i (Theorem 7.37), and rank(ATA)=rankA\operatorname{rank}(A^{T}A) = \operatorname{rank} A by Remark 9.51.

Theorem 10.24 (The singular value decomposition (SVD))¶

Let AA be an m×nm \times n matrix of rank rr, with positive singular values σ1≥⋯≥σr>0\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0. Then there exist orthogonal matrices U=(𝐮1⋯𝐮m)∈ℝm×mU = (\mathbf{u}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{u}_m) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m} and V=(𝐯1⋯𝐯n)∈ℝn×nV = (\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, and a matrix Σ=(Dr000)∈ℝm×n\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} D_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} with Dr=diag(σ1,…,σr)D_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r), so that A=UΣVT.A = U\Sigma V^{T} .(10.5)

By the spectral theorem applied to ATAA^{T}A, there is an orthonormal basis 𝐯1,…,𝐯n\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n with ATA𝐯i=λi𝐯iA^{T}A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i, ordered so that λ1≥⋯≥λn\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n; by Definition 10.23 λi=σi2>0\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2 > 0 for i≤ri \le r and λi=0\lambda_i = 0 for i>ri > r. For i>ri > r, ∥A𝐯i∥2=𝐯iTATA𝐯i=λi=0\|A\mathbf{v}_i\|^2 = \mathbf{v}_i^{T}A^{T}A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i = 0, so A𝐯i=0A\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}. For i≤ri \le r, put 𝐮i=A𝐯i/σi\mathbf{u}_i = A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i. These vectors are orthonormal: 𝐮i⋅𝐮j=(A𝐯i)T(A𝐯j)σiσj=𝐯iT(ATA𝐯j)σiσj=λj𝐯i⋅𝐯jσiσj={1i=j,0i≠j.\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j = \frac{(A\mathbf{v}_i)^{T}(A\mathbf{v}_j)}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{\mathbf{v}_i^{T}(A^{T}A\mathbf{v}_j)}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{\lambda_j\,\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j, \\ 0 & i \neq j. \end{cases} Extend 𝐮1,…,𝐮r\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_r to an orthonormal basis 𝐮1,…,𝐮m\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_m of ℝm\mathbb{R}^m (extend to a basis by Theorem 6.47, then apply Gram–Schmidt, which keeps the first rr vectors, and normalize). Proposition 10.27 (What the SVD tells us)¶

  2. (a) This was shown in Definition 10.23.

(b) By (10.6), 𝐯r+1,…,𝐯n\mathbf{v}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n lie in Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A); they are n−rn - r orthonormal, hence independent, vectors, and dimNul(A)=n−r\dim \operatorname{Nul}(A) = n - r by the rank theorem (Theorem 7.9), so they are a basis (Theorem 6.48). Remark 10.28 (The geometry of the SVD)¶

Read A𝐱=U(Σ(VT𝐱))A\mathbf{x} = U(\Sigma(V^{T}\mathbf{x})) from right to left: VTV^{T} takes the orthonormal basis 𝐯i\mathbf{v}_i to the standard basis without changing lengths or angles (Proposition 9.20); Σ\Sigma stretches the ii-th axis by σi\sigma_i (and drops or adds coordinates when m≠nm \neq n); UU takes the standard basis to the 𝐮i\mathbf{u}_i, again preserving lengths and angles. So, for an invertible n×nn \times n matrix, AA maps the unit sphere onto an ellipsoid with semi-axes σ1𝐮1,…,σn𝐮n\sigma_1\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \sigma_n\mathbf{u}_n. Part (c) of Proposition 10.27 is the statement that the longest semi-axis has length σ1\sigma_1, and part (d) matches Theorem 5.33: the volume of the ellipsoid is σ1⋯σn\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_n times that of the ball.

Remark 10.31 (Symmetric matrices, and computing the SVD)¶

If AA is symmetric with A=QDQTA = QDQ^{T}, then ATA=A2=QD2QTA^{T}A = A^2 = QD^2Q^{T}, so the singular values of AA are the absolute values |λi||\lambda_i| of its eigenvalues; for a positive semidefinite AA the spectral decomposition is an SVD. Computer programs do not form ATAA^{T}A at all, for the reason given in Remark 9.54; they compute the SVD directly by orthogonal transformations, and the SVD is the most reliable way to determine the rank of a matrix whose entries carry rounding errors: count the singular values that are not negligibly small.

10.3  The pseudoinverse and least squares

💡 Intuition

Lecture 9 solved least-squares problems when the columns of AA are independent, and left open what to do when they are not (Remark 9.59): then there are infinitely many least-squares solutions, and one wants the shortest. The SVD settles this in one stroke. In the orthonormal coordinates given by VV and UU, the matrix AA becomes the diagonal matrix Σ\Sigma, and for a diagonal matrix the best one can do is obvious: divide by the nonzero σi\sigma_i and set the other unknowns to 00. Translating back gives a matrix A†A^{\dagger}, the pseudoinverse, with A†𝐛A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b} the shortest least-squares solution.

Definition 10.32 (The pseudoinverse)¶

Let A=UΣVTA = U\Sigma V^{T} be an SVD of the m×nm \times n matrix AA of rank rr. The pseudoinverse (or Moore–Penrose inverse) of AA is the n×mn \times m matrix A†=VΣ†UT=1σ1𝐯1𝐮1T+⋯+1σr𝐯r𝐮rT=VrDr−1UrT,A^{\dagger} = V\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{T} = \frac{1}{\sigma_1}\mathbf{v}_1\mathbf{u}_1^{T} + \cdots + \frac{1}{\sigma_r}\mathbf{v}_r\mathbf{u}_r^{T} = V_rD_r^{-1}U_r^{T},(10.8) where Σ†\Sigma^{\dagger} is the n×mn \times m matrix with 1/σ1,…,1/σr1/\sigma_1, \dots, 1/\sigma_r in the positions (1,1),…,(r,r)(1,1), \dots, (r,r) and zeros elsewhere. (One can show that A†A^{\dagger} does not depend on the choice of the SVD; the next theorem gives a characterization that does not mention UU and VV.)

Theorem 10.33 (The shortest least-squares solution)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n and 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m, and let 𝐱†=A†𝐛\mathbf{x}^{\dagger} = A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}. Then:

  1. 𝐱†\mathbf{x}^{\dagger} is a least-squares solution of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b};
  2. 𝐱†∈Row(A)\mathbf{x}^{\dagger} \in \operatorname{Row}(A), and it is the only least-squares solution in Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A);
  3. every other least-squares solution is longer: if 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} is a least-squares solution and 𝐱̂≠𝐱†\hat{\mathbf{x}} \neq \mathbf{x}^{\dagger}, then ∥𝐱̂∥>∥𝐱†∥\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\| > \|\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}\|.

Change coordinates on both sides: let 𝐲=VT𝐱\mathbf{y} = V^{T}\mathbf{x} and 𝐜=UT𝐛\mathbf{c} = U^{T}\mathbf{b}, so ci=𝐮i⋅𝐛c_i = \mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}. Since UTU^{T} preserves lengths (Proposition 9.20) and UTA=ΣVTU^{T}A = \Sigma V^{T}, ∥A𝐱−𝐛∥2=∥UT(A𝐱−𝐛)∥2=∥Σ𝐲−𝐜∥2=∑i=1r(σiyi−ci)2+∑i=r+1mci2.\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|U^{T}(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})\|^2 = \|\Sigma\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{c}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} (\sigma_i y_i - c_i)^2 + \sum_{i=r+1}^{m} c_i^2 . So the least-squares solutions are the vectors 𝐱=V𝐲\mathbf{x} = V\mathbf{y} with yi=ci/σiy_i = c_i/\sigma_i for i≤ri \le r and yr+1,…,yny_{r+1}, \dots, y_n arbitrary, that is, 𝐱=∑i=1r𝐮i⋅𝐛σi𝐯i+∑i=r+1nyi𝐯i.\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i + \sum_{i=r+1}^{n} y_i\mathbf{v}_i . The first sum is VrDr−1UrT𝐛=A†𝐛V_rD_r^{-1}U_r^{T}\mathbf{b} = A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}, which proves (a). It lies in Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯r)=Row(A)\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r) = \operatorname{Row}(A), and the second sum lies in Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) (Proposition 10.27(b)); a least-squares solution lies in Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A) exactly when the second sum is 0\mathbf{0}, which proves (b). Proposition 10.34 (Properties of the pseudoinverse)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n of rank rr.

  1. AA†=UrUrTAA^{\dagger} = U_rU_r^{T} is the matrix of the orthogonal projection onto Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A), and A†A=VrVrTA^{\dagger}A = V_rV_r^{T} is the matrix of the orthogonal projection onto Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A).
  2. If the columns of AA are independent (r=nr = n), then A†=(ATA)−1ATA^{\dagger} = (A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}, and A†𝐛A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b} is the 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} of Lecture 9.

  4. If AA is square and invertible, then A†=A−1A^{\dagger} = A^{-1}.

(a) By (10.7) and (10.8), AA†=UrDrVrTVrDr−1UrT=UrUrT,AA^{\dagger} = U_rD_rV_r^{T}\,V_rD_r^{-1}U_r^{T} = U_rU_r^{T}, because VrTVr=IrV_r^{T}V_r = I_r (Lemma 9.15). (c) If r=mr = m, then Col(A)=ℝm\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m, so every 𝐛\mathbf{b} is in it and the least-squares solutions are the solutions. 10.4  Best approximation by matrices of low rank

💡 Intuition

The compact SVD writes AA as a sum of rr rank-one pieces σi𝐮i𝐯iT\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^{T}, ordered by importance. Keeping only the first kk pieces gives a matrix AkA_k of rank kk that is cheap to store, k(m+n+1)k(m + n + 1) numbers instead of mnmn, and the Eckart–Young theorem says that no matrix of rank kk is closer to AA. This is the mathematics behind image compression, behind the principal components of a data set, and behind the “latent factors” of recommendation systems. To speak of “closer” we first need the size of a matrix.

Definition 10.38 (Two norms of a matrix)¶

For an m×nm \times n matrix AA:

  1. the Frobenius norm is ∥A∥F=∑i,jaij2\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2}, the length of AA viewed as a vector of mnmn entries;
  2. the spectral norm (or operator norm) is ∥A∥2=max∥𝐱∥=1∥A𝐱∥\|A\|_2 = \max_{\|\mathbf{x}\| = 1}\|A\mathbf{x}\|, the largest factor by which AA stretches a vector.
The distance between two matrices AA and BB of the same size is ∥A−B∥F\|A - B\|_F or ∥A−B∥2\|A - B\|_2.

Proposition 10.39 (The norms from the singular values)¶

If AA has singular values σ1≥⋯≥σr>0\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0, then ∥A∥2=σ1,∥A∥F2=σ12+σ22+⋯+σr2.\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1, \qquad \|A\|_F^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \cdots + \sigma_r^2 .

The first is part (c) of Proposition 10.27. For the second, note that ∥B∥F2\|B\|_F^2 is the sum of the squared lengths of the columns of BB, and also of the rows of BB. If UU is orthogonal, the columns of UBUB are UU times the columns of BB and have the same lengths (Proposition 9.20), so ∥UB∥F=∥B∥F\|UB\|_F = \|B\|_F. Likewise the rows of BVTBV^{T} are the transposes of VV times the rows of BB, so ∥BVT∥F=∥B∥F\|BV^{T}\|_F = \|B\|_F for orthogonal VV. Theorem 10.41 (Eckart–Young: best approximation of low rank)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n with singular values σ1≥⋯≥σr>0\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0, let 0≤k<r0 \le k < r, and let AkA_k be as in (10.9).

So AkA_k is a closest matrix of rank at most kk to AA, in both norms.

Remark 10.45 (Looking back)¶

The course began with the system A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} and row reduction, which reveals the rank and the four subspaces of AA. The SVD reveals the same information, but with orthonormal bases and with the singular values measuring how strongly AA acts in each direction. That is why it is the tool of choice whenever the data are inexact: it gives the shortest least-squares solution, the numerical rank, and the best approximations of lower rank.
