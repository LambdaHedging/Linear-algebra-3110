6.1  Vector spaces

💡 Intuition

In Lecture 1 we listed eight rules obeyed by the addition and scaling of vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, and a remark promised that other objects obey the same rules. Matrices of a fixed size do (Lecture 4), and so do polynomials, and so do functions. Each time we prove a result from the eight rules alone, it holds for all of these at once. That is the point of the abstract definition below: one proof, many applications. The price is that we may use only the rules; coordinates and entries are no longer available unless we bring them in on purpose.

Definition 6.1 (Vector space)¶

A vector space (over ℝ\mathbb{R}) is a set VV together with two operations,

  • an addition, which assigns to any 𝐮,𝐯∈V\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V an element 𝐮+𝐯∈V\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in V, and
  • a scalar multiplication, which assigns to any c∈ℝc \in \mathbb{R} and 𝐮∈V\mathbf{u} \in V an element c𝐮∈Vc\,\mathbf{u} \in V,
such that for all 𝐮,𝐯,𝐰∈V\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V and all c,d∈ℝc, d \in \mathbb{R}:
  1. 𝐮+𝐯=𝐯+𝐮\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u};
  2. (𝐮+𝐯)+𝐰=𝐮+(𝐯+𝐰)(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w});
  3. there is an element 0∈V\mathbf{0} \in V, the zero vector, with 𝐮+0=𝐮\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u} for every 𝐮\mathbf{u};
  4. for each 𝐮\mathbf{u} there is an element −𝐮∈V-\mathbf{u} \in V with 𝐮+(−𝐮)=0\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0};
  5. c(𝐮+𝐯)=c𝐮+c𝐯c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v};
  6. (c+d)𝐮=c𝐮+d𝐮(c + d)\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u};
  7. c(d𝐮)=(cd)𝐮c(d\mathbf{u}) = (cd)\mathbf{u};
  8. 1𝐮=𝐮1\,\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}.
The elements of VV are called vectors, whatever they are; the real numbers are called scalars. The requirement that 𝐮+𝐯\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} and c𝐮c\,\mathbf{u} lie again in VV is called closure under the two operations.

Example 6.2 (The space ℝn\mathbb{R}^n)¶

With entrywise addition and scaling, ℝn\mathbb{R}^n is a vector space: the eight rules are the algebraic properties of Lecture 1. Its zero vector is the column of nn zeros. This is the model for everything that follows.

Example 6.3 (The space ℝm×n\mathbb{R}^{m \times n} of matrices)¶

Let ℝm×n\mathbb{R}^{m \times n} denote the set of all m×nm \times n real matrices, with the entrywise addition and scalar multiplication of Lecture 4. The eight rules are the arithmetic of matrix addition; the zero vector is the zero matrix. As Lecture 4 put it, for adding and scaling an m×nm \times n matrix is just a list of mnmn numbers arranged in a rectangle. (The matrix product is extra structure that a vector space does not need.)

Example 6.4 (The space ℙn\mathbb{P}_n of polynomials)¶

For an integer n≥1n \ge 1, let ℙn\mathbb{P}_n be the set of all polynomials in a variable tt of degree at most n−1n - 1, that is, with nn coefficients: p(t)=a0+a1t+a2t2+⋯+an−1tn−1,a0,a1,…,an−1∈ℝ.p(t) = a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2 + \cdots + a_{n-1} t^{n-1}, \qquad a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1} \in \mathbb{R} . Two polynomials are equal when they have the same coefficients. They are added, and multiplied by scalars, coefficient by coefficient: if q(t)=b0+b1t+⋯+bn−1tn−1q(t) = b_0 + b_1 t + \cdots + b_{n-1} t^{n-1}, then (p+q)(t)=(a0+b0)+(a1+b1)t+⋯+(an−1+bn−1)tn−1,(cp)(t)=ca0+ca1t+⋯+can−1tn−1.\begin{aligned} (p + q)(t) &= (a_0 + b_0) + (a_1 + b_1)t + \cdots + (a_{n-1} + b_{n-1})t^{n-1}, \\ (c\,p)(t) &= c a_0 + c a_1 t + \cdots + c a_{n-1} t^{n-1} . \end{aligned} The sum and multiples again have degree at most n−1n - 1, so these operations stay in ℙn\mathbb{P}_n, and each rule is a rule of real arithmetic applied to one coefficient at a time, exactly as for ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}. The zero vector is the zero polynomial, all of whose coefficients are 00. (We index by the number of coefficients, so that ℙn\mathbb{P}_n will turn out to have dimension nn: for instance ℙ3\mathbb{P}_3 consists of the polynomials a0+a1t+a2t2a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2 of degree at most 22.) For example, in ℙ3\mathbb{P}_3, (1−2t+t2)+3(2+t)=7+t+t2.(1 - 2t + t^2) + 3\,(2 + t) = 7 + t + t^2 .

Example 6.5 (Functions)¶

Let ℱ\mathcal{F} be the set of all functions f:ℝ→ℝf\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, added and scaled pointwise: (f+g)(t)=f(t)+g(t)(f + g)(t) = f(t) + g(t) and (cf)(t)=cf(t)(c f)(t) = c\,f(t), just as linear maps were added in Lecture 4. Each rule holds because it holds for the real numbers f(t)f(t), g(t)g(t) at every single tt; the zero vector is the function that is 00 everywhere. So ℱ\mathcal{F} is a vector space. Its vectors include sint\sin t, ete^{t} and |t||t|; signals, in engineering language.

Inside ℱ\mathcal{F} sit the continuous functions. With the same operations they form a vector space C(ℝ)C(\mathbb{R}) of their own: the zero function is continuous, and sums and scalar multiples of continuous functions are continuous, by the limit rules of calculus. The same holds for the differentiable functions. (In the language of the next section, these are subspaces of ℱ\mathcal{F}.)

Proposition 6.6 (Consequences of the rules)¶

  1. the zero vector is unique, and so is the negative −𝐮-\mathbf{u};
  2. 0𝐮=00\,\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0} and c0=0c\,\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0};
  3. (−1)𝐮=−𝐮(-1)\,\mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u};
  4. if c𝐮=0c\,\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}, then c=0c = 0 or 𝐮=0\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}.

We use the rules by number. Remark 6.7 (Why bother with the proofs)¶

In ℝn\mathbb{R}^n each statement of the proposition is obvious from the entries. The point of the proofs is that they use only the eight rules, so the statements hold in ℝm×n\mathbb{R}^{m \times n}, in ℙn\mathbb{P}_n, in ℱ\mathcal{F}, and in every vector space we will ever meet, with no further checking. We write 𝐮−𝐯\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} for 𝐮+(−𝐯)\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v}).

Example 6.8 (Sets that are not vector spaces)¶

Closure can fail. The polynomials of degree exactly 22 (with the usual operations) do not form a vector space: t2t^2 and −t2+t-t^2 + t have degree 22, but their sum tt does not; and the zero polynomial is missing altogether. The set of vectors in ℝ2\mathbb{R}^2 with both entries ≥0\ge 0 is closed under addition, but not under scalar multiplication: (−1)(1,2)T(-1)(1,\, 2)^{T} falls outside it.

6.2  Subspaces and span

💡 Intuition

Most vector spaces we meet sit inside a bigger one: a plane through the origin inside ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3, the symmetric matrices inside ℝn×n\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, the polynomials of degree at most 22 inside those of degree at most 55. For such a subset, the eight rules come for free from the big space; only closure has to be checked. And the most important way to manufacture subspaces is to start with a few vectors and take all their linear combinations: their span.

Definition 6.9 (Subspace)¶

A subspace of a vector space VV is a subset H⊆VH \subseteq V such that

  1. 0∈H\mathbf{0} \in H;
  2. HH is closed under addition: if 𝐮,𝐯∈H\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in H, then 𝐮+𝐯∈H\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in H;
  3. HH is closed under scalar multiplication: if 𝐮∈H\mathbf{u} \in H and c∈ℝc \in \mathbb{R}, then c𝐮∈Hc\,\mathbf{u} \in H.

Proposition 6.10 (A subspace is a vector space)¶

Every subspace HH of VV, with the operations of VV, is itself a vector space.

By (2) and (3) the operations of VV applied to elements of HH stay in HH. Rules 1, 2 and 5–8 are equations between elements of VV, true for all elements of VV, hence for those of HH. Rule 3 holds since 0∈H\mathbf{0} \in H. For rule 4: if 𝐮∈H\mathbf{u} \in H, then −𝐮=(−1)𝐮∈H-\mathbf{u} = (-1)\mathbf{u} \in H by the proposition on consequences and (3).

Example 6.11 (Subspaces of ℝ2\mathbb{R}^2 and ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3)¶

Every vector space VV has the two extreme subspaces {0}\{\mathbf{0}\} (the zero subspace) and VV itself. In ℝ2\mathbb{R}^2, every line through the origin, L={c𝐮:c∈ℝ}L = \{\, c\,\mathbf{u} : c \in \mathbb{R} \,\} with 𝐮≠0\mathbf{u} \neq \mathbf{0}, is a subspace: 0=0𝐮\mathbf{0} = 0\,\mathbf{u}, c𝐮+d𝐮=(c+d)𝐮c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u} = (c + d)\mathbf{u}, and k(c𝐮)=(kc)𝐮k(c\mathbf{u}) = (kc)\mathbf{u}. In ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3, lines and planes through the origin are subspaces (we will see why in the next examples); lines and planes that miss the origin are not.

Example 6.12 (Subspaces of matrices and polynomials)¶

In ℝn×n\mathbb{R}^{n \times n} the symmetric matrices (AT=AA^{T} = A) form a subspace: 0T=0\mathbf{0}^{T} = \mathbf{0}, (A+B)T=AT+BT=A+B(A + B)^{T} = A^{T} + B^{T} = A + B and (cA)T=cAT=cA(cA)^{T} = cA^{T} = cA. So do the upper triangular matrices, and the diagonal ones. But the invertible matrices do not: the zero matrix is not invertible.

In ℙ4\mathbb{P}_4, the polynomials with p(1)=0p(1) = 0 form a subspace: the zero polynomial vanishes at 11, and if p(1)=q(1)=0p(1) = q(1) = 0, then (p+q)(1)=0(p + q)(1) = 0 and (cp)(1)=0(c\,p)(1) = 0. The polynomials with p(1)=5p(1) = 5 do not. Also ℙ3\mathbb{P}_3 is a subspace of ℙ4\mathbb{P}_4. Inside ℱ\mathcal{F} we have the chain of subspaces ℙn⊆C(ℝ)⊆ℱ\mathbb{P}_n \subseteq C(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}: polynomials are continuous functions, and the continuous functions form a subspace of ℱ\mathcal{F} by the example on functions.

Definition 6.13 (Linear combination and span)¶

Let 𝐯1,…,𝐯k\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k be vectors in a vector space VV. A linear combination of them is a vector c1𝐯1+c2𝐯2+⋯+ck𝐯k,c1,…,ck∈ℝ,c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k , \qquad c_1, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} , and their span is the set of all such combinations: Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯k)={c1𝐯1+⋯+ck𝐯k:c1,…,ck∈ℝ}.\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k) = \{\, c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k : c_1, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \,\}. If Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯k)=H\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k) = H, we say that 𝐯1,…,𝐯k\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k span (or generate) HH, and call them a spanning set of HH. By convention the span of no vectors at all is {0}\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Theorem 6.14 (A span is a subspace)¶

For any 𝐯1,…,𝐯k∈V\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k \in V, the set H=Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯k)H = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k) is a subspace of VV. It is the smallest subspace containing 𝐯1,…,𝐯k\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k: every subspace that contains these vectors contains HH.

0=0𝐯1+⋯+0𝐯k∈H\mathbf{0} = 0\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + 0\mathbf{v}_k \in H (using 0𝐯=00\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}). If 𝐮=∑ici𝐯i\mathbf{u} = \sum_i c_i \mathbf{v}_i and 𝐰=∑idi𝐯i\mathbf{w} = \sum_i d_i \mathbf{v}_i, then by the rules of a vector space 𝐮+𝐰=∑i(ci+di)𝐯i∈H\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = \sum_i (c_i + d_i)\mathbf{v}_i \in H and a𝐮=∑i(aci)𝐯i∈Ha\,\mathbf{u} = \sum_i (a c_i)\mathbf{v}_i \in H. Finally, a subspace containing every 𝐯i\mathbf{v}_i contains every ci𝐯ic_i\mathbf{v}_i by closure under scaling, and then every sum of these by closure under addition; that is, it contains HH.

Remark 6.18 (Any vector may be replaced)¶

A span does not depend on the order in which the vectors are listed, so the proposition applies to any one of the vectors: replacing 𝐯j\mathbf{v}_j by 𝐯j\mathbf{v}_j plus a linear combination of the other vectors does not change the span. For example, Span(𝐮,𝐯)=Span(𝐮,𝐯+3𝐮)=Span(𝐮−2𝐯,𝐯).\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} + 3\mathbf{u}) = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{u} - 2\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v}) . This is the row operation “add a multiple of one row to another” in the language of spans; Lecture 7 uses it for the row space of a matrix.

Corollary 6.19 (Removing a redundant vector)¶

If 𝐯k\mathbf{v}_k is a linear combination of 𝐯1,…,𝐯k−1\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k-1}, then Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯k−1,𝐯k)=Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯k−1).\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k-1}, \mathbf{v}_k) = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k-1}).

Write 𝐯k=a1𝐯1+⋯+ak−1𝐯k−1\mathbf{v}_k = a_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + a_{k-1}\mathbf{v}_{k-1} and apply the proposition with ci=−aic_i = -a_i; then 𝐰=0\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}, so the span equals Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯k−1,0)\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k-1}, \mathbf{0}). 6.3  The null space, column space, and row space of a matrix

💡 Intuition

Every m×nm \times n matrix AA carries three subspaces, and together they record everything we have learned about A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}. The null space, inside ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, holds the solutions of the homogeneous system; the column space, inside ℝm\mathbb{R}^m, holds the right-hand sides 𝐛\mathbf{b} for which a solution exists; and the row space, inside ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, holds all combinations of the equations. One is described by equations, the other two by generators, and passing between the two descriptions is a matter of row reduction.

Definition 6.20 (Null space)¶

The null space (or kernel, or solution space) of an m×nm \times n matrix AA is the set of all solutions of the homogeneous system A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}: Nul(A)={𝐱∈ℝn:A𝐱=0}.\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \,\}.

Theorem 6.21 (The null space is a subspace)¶

For every m×nm \times n matrix AA, Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) is a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

A0=0A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}, so 0∈Nul(A)\mathbf{0} \in \operatorname{Nul}(A). If A𝐮=0A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0} and A𝐯=0A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}, then by linearity A(𝐮+𝐯)=A𝐮+A𝐯=0A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} and A(c𝐮)=cA𝐮=0A(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}.

□
Remark 6.22 (Solutions of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} with 𝐛≠0\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0})¶

If 𝐛≠0\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}, the solution set of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is not a subspace, since 0\mathbf{0} is not a solution. By the structure theorem of Lecture 3 it is Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) translated by a particular solution 𝐩\mathbf{p}: a line or a plane that has been shifted off the origin.

Example 6.23 (A null space as a span)¶

For the matrix of Lecture 3's homogeneous example, A=(1203241836111),A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 11 \end{pmatrix}, row reduction gave the parametric vector form of the solutions, so Nul(A)=Span((−2100),(−30−21))⊆ℝ4.\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \operatorname{Span}\left( \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4 . This is the general recipe: solving A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} and writing the solution in parametric vector form turns the equations that define Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) into a spanning set, one vector for each free variable.

Definition 6.24 (Column space and row space)¶

Let AA be an m×nm \times n matrix with columns 𝐚1,…,𝐚n∈ℝm\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n \in \mathbb{R}^m and rows 𝐫1,…,𝐫m\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_m.

  • The column space of AA is the span of its columns: Col(A)=Span(𝐚1,…,𝐚n)⊆ℝm\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m.
  • The row space of AA is the span of its rows, each written as a column vector: Row(A)=Span(𝐫1T,…,𝐫mT)⊆ℝn\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{r}_1^{T}, \dots, \mathbf{r}_m^{T}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n. In other words, Row(A)=Col(AT)\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^{T}).
Both are subspaces, by the theorem on spans.

Theorem 6.25 (What the column space measures)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n. Then Col(A)={A𝐱:𝐱∈ℝn},\operatorname{Col}(A) = \{\, A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \,\}, and a vector 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m lies in Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) if and only if A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is consistent. Moreover Col(A)=ℝm\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m if and only if an echelon form of AA has a pivot in every row.

By the column view, A𝐱=x1𝐚1+⋯+xn𝐚nA\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n; as 𝐱\mathbf{x} runs over ℝn\mathbb{R}^n this runs over all linear combinations of the columns. The second statement is a restatement of the first. The last one is part (2) of the theorem on surjectivity: the map 𝐱↦A𝐱\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x} is onto exactly when every row has a pivot.

Remark 6.26 (Kernel and image)¶

For the linear map T(𝐱)=A𝐱T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} from ℝn\mathbb{R}^n to ℝm\mathbb{R}^m, Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) is the set of inputs sent to 0\mathbf{0}, called the kernel of TT, and Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) is the set of all outputs, called the image (or range) of TT. By Lecture 3, TT is injective exactly when Nul(A)={0}\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}, and surjective exactly when Col(A)=ℝm\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m.

Remark 6.28 (Row and column operations)¶

By the proposition on replacing a vector, the row space of a matrix does not change when a row operation is performed. Write the rows of AA (as columns) 𝐫1,…,𝐫m\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_m.

  • A replacement changes 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_i into 𝐫i+c𝐫k\mathbf{r}_i + c\,\mathbf{r}_k (k≠ik \neq i), which is exactly the situation of the proposition (see the remark after it).
  • A swap only changes the order of the spanning vectors.
  • A scaling changes 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_i into c𝐫ic\,\mathbf{r}_i with c≠0c \neq 0; a combination with weight aa on 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_i is the same as one with weight a/ca/c on c𝐫ic\,\mathbf{r}_i.
So every matrix obtained from AA by row operations, an echelon form in particular, has the same row space as AA. In the example, the reduced echelon form has nonzero rows (1,2,0,3)(1, 2, 0, 3) and (0,0,1,2)(0, 0, 1, 2), and indeed these span Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A), since (2,4,1,8)=2(1,2,0,3)+(0,0,1,2).(2, 4, 1, 8) = 2\,(1, 2, 0, 3) + (0, 0, 1, 2) .

In the same way, the column space does not change under column operations: swapping two columns, multiplying a column by a nonzero scalar, or adding a multiple of one column to another.

Row operations, however, usually do change the column space (and column operations the row space). In the example, every column of the reduced echelon form has third entry 00, while the column (1,2,3)T(1, 2, 3)^{T} of AA does not; the two column spaces are different planes. Lecture 7 shows how an echelon form still tells us which columns of AA form a basis of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A).

Remark 6.29 (Two ways to describe a subspace)¶

Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) is described implicitly, by equations: testing whether a given 𝐱\mathbf{x} belongs to it is a single product A𝐱A\mathbf{x}, but producing its vectors requires solving a system. Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) and Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A) are described explicitly, by generators: producing their vectors is easy (take any combination), but testing membership requires solving a system. Row reduction converts one description into the other.

6.4  Linear independence, bases, and coordinates

💡 Intuition

A spanning set may contain redundant vectors, and redundancy is exactly linear dependence, as in Lecture 3. A spanning set with no redundancy is a basis. Bases are the coordinate systems of a vector space: once a basis is fixed, every vector has a unique list of coordinates, and every question about an abstract space (ℙn\mathbb{P}_n, matrices, subspaces) becomes a question about columns of numbers, which row reduction can answer.

Definition 6.30 (Linear independence)¶

Vectors 𝐯1,…,𝐯k\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k in a vector space VV are linearly independent if the only scalars with c1𝐯1+c2𝐯2+⋯+ck𝐯k=0c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0} are c1=c2=⋯=ck=0c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0. Otherwise they are linearly dependent, and an equation of this form with some ci≠0c_i \neq 0 is a dependence relation. For V=ℝmV = \mathbb{R}^m this is the definition of Lecture 3.

Example 6.31 (Independence outside ℝn\mathbb{R}^n)¶

In ℙ3\mathbb{P}_3 the polynomials 1,t,t21, t, t^2 are independent: c0+c1t+c2t2c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 is the zero polynomial only if all its coefficients are 00. The polynomials 1+t1 + t, 1−t1 - t, tt are dependent, because (1+t)−(1−t)−2t=0.(1 + t) - (1 - t) - 2t = 0 . In ℱ\mathcal{F} the functions sint\sin t and cost\cos t are independent: if c1sint+c2cost=0c_1 \sin t + c_2 \cos t = 0 for every tt, then t=0t = 0 gives c2=0c_2 = 0 and t=π/2t = \pi/2 gives c1=0c_1 = 0.

Proposition 6.32 (Dependence means redundancy)¶

Let k≥2k \ge 2. The vectors 𝐯1,…,𝐯k\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k are linearly dependent if and only if at least one of them is a linear combination of the others. Also, a single vector is independent if and only if it is nonzero, and any list containing 0\mathbf{0} is dependent.

If c1𝐯1+⋯+ck𝐯k=0c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0} with cj≠0c_j \neq 0, solve for 𝐯j\mathbf{v}_j: 𝐯j=−∑i≠j(ci/cj)𝐯i\mathbf{v}_j = -\sum_{i \neq j} (c_i / c_j)\,\mathbf{v}_i. Conversely, if 𝐯j=∑i≠jai𝐯i\mathbf{v}_j = \sum_{i \neq j} a_i \mathbf{v}_i, then ∑i≠jai𝐯i−𝐯j=0\sum_{i \neq j} a_i\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{0} is a dependence relation, with coefficient −1-1 on 𝐯j\mathbf{v}_j. For a single vector, c𝐯=0c\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} with c≠0c \neq 0 happens exactly when 𝐯=0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}, by the proposition on consequences; and 1⋅0=01 \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} is a dependence relation for any list containing 0\mathbf{0}.

Definition 6.33 (Basis)¶

A basis of a vector space VV is a list ℬ=(𝐛1,…,𝐛n)\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n) of vectors in VV that

  1. is linearly independent, and
  2. spans VV: Span(𝐛1,…,𝐛n)=V\operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n) = V.
The order of the vectors is part of the data, because it fixes the order of the coordinates below. The zero space {0}\{\mathbf{0}\} has the empty list as its basis, since the span of no vectors is {0}\{\mathbf{0}\}. Because a subspace HH of VV is itself a vector space (the proposition on subspaces), this also defines a basis of HH: a list of vectors in HH that is independent and spans HH.

Convention. In this course we consider only vector spaces that have a basis consisting of finitely many vectors (the finite-dimensional spaces, as they will be called in the next section). Spaces such as ℱ\mathcal{F} and C(ℝ)C(\mathbb{R}), which are not spanned by any finite list of vectors, are not treated further.

Theorem 6.37 (Unique representation)¶

A list ℬ=(𝐛1,…,𝐛n)\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n) of vectors in a vector space VV is a basis of VV if and only if every 𝐯∈V\mathbf{v} \in V can be written as 𝐯=c1𝐛1+c2𝐛2+⋯+cn𝐛n\mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{b}_1 + c_2\mathbf{b}_2 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf{b}_n for exactly one choice of scalars c1,…,cnc_1, \dots, c_n.

Conversely, if every 𝐯∈V\mathbf{v} \in V has exactly one representation, then ℬ\mathcal{B} spans VV; and 0\mathbf{0} has the representation with all ci=0c_i = 0, so by uniqueness it has no other: ℬ\mathcal{B} is independent.

Let ℬ\mathcal{B} be a basis of VV with nn vectors. For all 𝐮,𝐯∈V\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V and c∈ℝc \in \mathbb{R}, [𝐮+𝐯]ℬ=[𝐮]ℬ+[𝐯]ℬ,[c𝐮]ℬ=c[𝐮]ℬ.[\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\mathbf{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} + [\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}, \qquad [c\,\mathbf{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} = c\,[\mathbf{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} . If 𝐮=∑ci𝐛i\mathbf{u} = \sum c_i\mathbf{b}_i and 𝐯=∑di𝐛i\mathbf{v} = \sum d_i\mathbf{b}_i, then 𝐮+𝐯=∑(ci+di)𝐛i\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \sum (c_i + d_i)\mathbf{b}_i and c𝐮=∑(cci)𝐛ic\,\mathbf{u} = \sum (c\,c_i)\mathbf{b}_i; by uniqueness these are the coordinates. 6.5  Dimension

💡 Intuition

A plane has many bases, but each consists of two vectors; ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3 has many bases, each of three vectors. The number of vectors in a basis does not depend on the basis, and that number is the dimension. The key fact behind it is a comparison: in a space spanned by mm vectors, any n>mn > m vectors are dependent. It is the abstract form of Lecture 3's corollary that more than mm vectors in ℝm\mathbb{R}^m are dependent, and its proof reduces to it.

Theorem 6.43 (Independent lists are no longer than spanning lists)¶

Suppose V=Span(𝐯1,…,𝐯m)V = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_m). Then any nn vectors 𝐰1,…,𝐰n∈V\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n \in V with n>mn > m are linearly dependent.

Each 𝐰j\mathbf{w}_j lies in the span of the 𝐯i\mathbf{v}_i, so we can write 𝐰j=a1j𝐯1+a2j𝐯2+⋯+amj𝐯m,j=1,…,n,\mathbf{w}_j = a_{1j}\mathbf{v}_1 + a_{2j}\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + a_{mj}\mathbf{v}_m , \qquad j = 1, \dots, n , and collect the coefficients in an m×nm \times n matrix A=(aij)A = (a_{ij}). Corollary 6.44 (All bases have the same size)¶

If a vector space VV has a basis of nn vectors, then every basis of VV has exactly nn vectors.

Let (𝐛1,…,𝐛n)(\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n) and (𝐝1,…,𝐝k)(\mathbf{d}_1, \dots, \mathbf{d}_k) be bases of VV. The 𝐝\mathbf{d}'s are independent and lie in the span of the nn vectors 𝐛i\mathbf{b}_i, so k≤nk \le n by the theorem. Exchanging the roles gives n≤kn \le k.

□
Definition 6.45 (Dimension)¶

If a vector space VV has a basis of nn vectors, VV is called finite-dimensional, and its dimension is dimV=n\dim V = n; by the corollary this number does not depend on the basis. The zero space has dimension 00 (its basis is the empty list). A vector space that is not spanned by any finite list of vectors is called infinite-dimensional; as agreed in the definition of a basis, we do not study such spaces.

Theorem 6.47 (Reducing and extending to a basis)¶

Let VV be a finite-dimensional vector space and HH a subspace of VV.

  1. Every finite list spanning HH contains a basis of HH: remove vectors that are combinations of the others until the list is independent.
  2. Every linearly independent list in HH can be extended to a basis of HH. In particular HH is finite-dimensional and dimH≤dimV\dim H \le \dim V.
  3. If dimH=dimV\dim H = \dim V, then H=VH = V.

(1) If the list is dependent, the proposition on redundancy provides a vector that is a combination of the others, and removing it does not change the span, by the corollary on redundant vectors. Repeat; the process stops, since the list gets shorter, and it stops at an independent list that still spans HH.

(2) Let 𝐮1,…,𝐮k∈H\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k \in H be independent. If they span HH, they are a basis. If not, choose 𝐰∈H\mathbf{w} \in H outside their span. Then 𝐮1,…,𝐮k,𝐰\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k, \mathbf{w} are still independent: in a relation c1𝐮1+⋯+ck𝐮k+c𝐰=0c_1\mathbf{u}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{u}_k + c\,\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0} we must have c=0c = 0 (otherwise 𝐰\mathbf{w} would be a combination of the 𝐮i\mathbf{u}_i), and then all ci=0c_i = 0. Repeat. All lists produced are independent lists in VV, and VV is spanned by dimV\dim V vectors, so by the theorem no list has more than dimV\dim V vectors; the process must stop, and it stops at a basis of HH with at most dimV\dim V vectors.

(3) A basis of HH is an independent list of dimV\dim V vectors in VV. If it did not span VV, part (2), applied with H=VH = V, would extend it to a basis of VV with more than dimV\dim V vectors, contradicting the corollary. So it spans VV, and H=VH = V.

□
Theorem 6.48 (The basis test)¶

Let VV be a vector space with dimV=n≥1\dim V = n \ge 1. Then

  1. any nn linearly independent vectors in VV form a basis of VV;
  2. any nn vectors that span VV form a basis of VV.

(1) By part (2) of the previous theorem, applied with H=VH = V, the nn vectors extend to a basis of VV, which has exactly nn vectors by the corollary; so nothing was added. (2) By part (1) of the previous theorem, again with H=VH = V, the nn vectors contain a basis, which has exactly nn vectors; so nothing was removed.

□
