9.1  When A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} has no solution

💡 Intuition

So far an inconsistent system has been a dead end: row reduction produces a row (0⋯0∣c)(0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid c) with c≠0c \neq 0, and we stop. In applications this is where the work begins. Measurements carry noise, a model is only approximately right, and numbers stored in a computer are rounded. A system built from such data typically has more equations than unknowns, and then it almost never has an exact solution. Yet the data still contain information, and we want the 𝐱\mathbf{x} that fits them best. This lecture makes “best” precise: we measure the error vector 𝐛−A𝐱\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} by its length and make it as short as possible. The answer is geometric, the closest point of a subspace, and it leads to three practical methods of computing it.

Example 9.1 (Measuring one quantity three times)¶

A length xx is measured three times, with results 1.101.10, 0.990.99 and 0.980.98. The “system” is x=1.10,x=0.99,x=0.98,that is,(111)x=(1.100.990.98),x = 1.10, \qquad x = 0.99, \qquad x = 0.98, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} x = \begin{pmatrix} 1.10 \\ 0.99 \\ 0.98 \end{pmatrix}, and it is plainly inconsistent. Nobody would answer ``there is no length''; the natural answer is a compromise, such as the average (1.10+0.99+0.98)/3=3.07/3≈1.023(1.10 + 0.99 + 0.98)/3 = 3.07/3 \approx 1.023. We will see that the average is exactly what the least-squares method produces (Section 9.6).

Example 9.2 (Fitting a line to data)¶

Suppose yy is a quantity that changes over time tt, and we want to know whether there is a linear trend in yy in terms of tt. An experiment gives four data points (t,y)(t, y): (0,1),(1,1),(2,2),(3,4).(0,\, 1), \qquad (1,\, 1), \qquad (2,\, 2), \qquad (3,\, 4). If the line passed through all four points, each point would give one linear equation in the unknowns c0,c1c_0, c_1: {c0=1c0+c1=1c0+2c1=2c0+3c1=4that is,(10111213)(c0c1)=(1124).\left\{ \begin{aligned} c_0 &= 1 \\ c_0 + c_1 &= 1 \\ c_0 + 2c_1 &= 2 \\ c_0 + 3c_1 &= 4 \end{aligned} \right. \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}. Remark 9.3 (Why exact solutions are rare)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n with m>nm > n, more equations than unknowns. The system A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is consistent exactly when 𝐛∈Col(A)\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A), by Theorem 6.25, and dimCol(A)=rankA≤n<m\dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{rank} A \le n < m, by the rank theorem (Theorem 7.9). So Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) is a proper subspace of ℝm\mathbb{R}^m, a line or a plane or a thinner set inside a bigger space. Even if the true data 𝐛\mathbf{b} lie in Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A), a small error in the measurements, or the rounding of each entry to the digits a computer keeps, moves 𝐛\mathbf{b} off this thin subspace in almost every case. Rounding the entries of AA has the same effect, since it changes the subspace itself. So with real data we must expect A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} to have no solution.

Remark 9.4 (The plan)¶

For each 𝐱\mathbf{x}, the vector 𝐛−A𝐱\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} is the error, also called the residual. We will choose 𝐱\mathbf{x} to make the residual as short as possible. As 𝐱\mathbf{x} varies, A𝐱A\mathbf{x} runs through all of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A), so the task is: find the vector of the subspace Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) that is closest to 𝐛\mathbf{b}. This needs a notion of length (Section 9.2), of perpendicularity and the closest point of a subspace (Sections 9.3 and 9.4), and good bases for computing it (Section 9.5). Section 9.6 returns to A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}.

9.2  Inner product, length, and orthogonality

💡 Intuition

Lecture 1 defined the row–column product, which multiplies a row by a column and gives a number. Pairing a column vector with another column vector in the same way, by turning the first one into a row, gives the inner product. In the plane and in space it is the familiar dot product, and it carries all the geometry we need: the length of a vector comes from Pythagoras, the distance between two points is the length of their difference, and two vectors are perpendicular when their inner product is 00. All of this makes sense in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n for every nn, where we can no longer draw, and the algebra lets us reason there as if we could.

Definition 9.5 (Inner product)¶

The inner product (or dot product) of 𝐮,𝐯∈ℝn\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n is the number 𝐮⋅𝐯=𝐮T𝐯=u1v1+u2v2+⋯+unvn,\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \cdots + u_n v_n , the row–column product (Definition 1.8) of the row vector 𝐮T\mathbf{u}^{T} with the column vector 𝐯\mathbf{v}. (For n=3n = 3 it appeared in Remark 5.15, on the cross product.)

Proposition 9.6 (Properties of the inner product)¶

For all 𝐮,𝐯,𝐰∈ℝn\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n and every scalar cc:

  1. 𝐮⋅𝐯=𝐯⋅𝐮\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u};
  2. (𝐮+𝐯)⋅𝐰=𝐮⋅𝐰+𝐯⋅𝐰(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} and (c𝐮)⋅𝐯=c(𝐮⋅𝐯)(c\,\mathbf{u}) \cdot \mathbf{v} = c\,(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}); by (a) the same holds in the second factor;
  3. 𝐮⋅𝐮≥0\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} \ge 0, and 𝐮⋅𝐮=0\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 if and only if 𝐮=0\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}.

(a) and (b) are statements about the sums ∑iuivi\sum_i u_i v_i and follow from the rules of arithmetic: uivi=viuiu_i v_i = v_i u_i, (ui+vi)wi=uiwi+viwi(u_i + v_i)w_i = u_i w_i + v_i w_i and (cui)vi=c(uivi)(c u_i)v_i = c(u_i v_i). Definition 9.7 (Length, distance, unit vector)¶

The length (or Euclidean norm) of 𝐮∈ℝn\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n is ∥𝐮∥=𝐮⋅𝐮=u12+u22+⋯+un2,\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}} = \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2 + \cdots + u_n^2} , which makes sense by part (c) of Proposition 9.6. The distance between 𝐮\mathbf{u} and 𝐯\mathbf{v} is dist(𝐮,𝐯)=∥𝐮−𝐯∥\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|. A vector of length 11 is a unit vector. For 𝐮≠0\mathbf{u} \neq \mathbf{0}, the vector 𝐮/∥𝐮∥\mathbf{u} / \|\mathbf{u}\| is a unit vector in the direction of 𝐮\mathbf{u}; replacing 𝐮\mathbf{u} by it is called normalizing 𝐮\mathbf{u}.

Remark 9.8 (Why this is the right length)¶

For 𝐮=(u1,u2)T\mathbf{u} = (u_1,\, u_2)^{T} in the plane, the arrow from 0\mathbf{0} to 𝐮\mathbf{u} is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs |u1||u_1| and |u2||u_2|, so its length is u12+u22\sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2} by Pythagoras; in space, Pythagoras applied twice gives u12+u22+u32\sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2 + u_3^2}. Definition 9.7 uses the same formula in every dimension. Since the ii-th entry of c𝐮c\,\mathbf{u} is cuic u_i, ∥c𝐮∥=c2u12+⋯+c2un2=|c|∥𝐮∥:\|c\,\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{c^2 u_1^2 + \cdots + c^2 u_n^2} = |c|\,\|\mathbf{u}\| : scaling a vector by cc scales its length by |c||c|. In particular ∥𝐮/∥𝐮∥∥=1\|\mathbf{u}/\|\mathbf{u}\|\| = 1, as claimed in Definition 9.7.

Definition 9.10 (Orthogonal vectors)¶

Two vectors 𝐮,𝐯∈ℝn\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n are orthogonal (or perpendicular), written 𝐮⊥𝐯\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}, if 𝐮⋅𝐯=0\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

Definition 9.14 (Orthogonal sets, matrices and bases)¶

Let 𝐮1,…,𝐮k\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k be vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

  1. The vectors 𝐮1,…,𝐮k\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k form an orthogonal set if 𝐮i⋅𝐮j=0\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j = 0 for all i≠ji \neq j, and an orthonormal set if moreover each 𝐮i\mathbf{u}_i is a unit vector.
  2. An n×kn \times k matrix is column orthogonal if its columns form an orthogonal set, and column orthonormal if its columns form an orthonormal set. A square column orthonormal matrix is called an orthogonal matrix.
  3. A basis of a vector space (in this lecture, of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n or of a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n) is an orthogonal basis if it is an orthogonal set, and an orthonormal basis if it is an orthonormal set.

Lemma 9.15 (The matrix HTHH^{T}H records all inner products)¶

Theorem 9.17 (Orthogonal sets are independent)¶

Let 𝐮1,…,𝐮k\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k be an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, and let W=Span(𝐮1,…,𝐮k)W = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k). Then:

  1. 𝐮1,…,𝐮k\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k are linearly independent, so they form an orthogonal basis of WW;
  2. every 𝐲∈W\mathbf{y} \in W has a unique representation 𝐲=c1𝐮1+⋯+ck𝐮k,wherecj=𝐲⋅𝐮j𝐮j⋅𝐮j,1≤j≤k.\mathbf{y} = c_1\mathbf{u}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{u}_k , \qquad\text{where}\qquad c_j = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}_j}{\mathbf{u}_j \cdot \mathbf{u}_j}, \quad 1 \le j \le k .

First we prove (b). Let 𝐲∈W\mathbf{y} \in W. 9.3  The orthogonal complement and the projection theorem

💡 Intuition

Here is the problem left open in Section 9.1. We are given a subspace WW of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n (for least squares, W=Col(A)W = \operatorname{Col}(A)) and a vector 𝐛\mathbf{b}, usually not in WW, and we want the vector 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}} of WW that is closest to 𝐛\mathbf{b}. The figure below shows the answer when WW is a plane in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3: drop the perpendicular from 𝐛\mathbf{b} to the plane. Its foot is 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}}, and 𝐛\mathbf{b} splits into two pieces, 𝐛=𝐛̂+𝐳,𝐛̂∈W,𝐳⊥every vector of W.\mathbf{b} = \hat{\mathbf{b}} + \mathbf{z}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{b}} \in W, \qquad \mathbf{z} \perp \text{every vector of } W . Why is 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}} the closest point? For any other point 𝐰\mathbf{w} of WW, the points 𝐛\mathbf{b}, 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}}, 𝐰\mathbf{w} form a right triangle with the right angle at 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}}, so the segment from 𝐛\mathbf{b} to 𝐰\mathbf{w} is a hypotenuse and is longer than 𝐳\mathbf{z}. In ℝn\mathbb{R}^n we cannot draw, so we prove both facts. In this section we name the set W⊥W^{\perp} of all vectors perpendicular to WW, describe it as a null space, and prove that every 𝐛\mathbf{b} splits uniquely as 𝐛̂+𝐳\hat{\mathbf{b}} + \mathbf{z} with 𝐛̂∈W\hat{\mathbf{b}} \in W and 𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp}; the proof also computes 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}} by solving a small square system. Section 9.4 then shows, with Pythagoras, that 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}} is the closest point.

Definition 9.21 (Orthogonal complement)¶

Let WW be a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n. A vector 𝐳\mathbf{z} is orthogonal to WW if 𝐳⋅𝐰=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w} = 0 for every 𝐰∈W\mathbf{w} \in W. The set of all such vectors is the orthogonal complement of WW, W⊥={𝐳∈ℝn:𝐳⋅𝐰=0 for all 𝐰∈W}W^{\perp} = \{\, \mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w} = 0 \text{ for all } \mathbf{w} \in W \,\} (read “WW perp”).

Proposition 9.22 (Computing W⊥W^{\perp})¶

Let W=Span(𝐰1,…,𝐰k)W = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k) be a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, and let B=(𝐰1⋯𝐰k)B = (\mathbf{w}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{w}_k) be the n×kn \times k matrix with these columns. Then:

  1. 𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp} if and only if 𝐳⋅𝐰i=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_i = 0 for i=1,…,ki = 1, \dots, k;
  2. W⊥=Nul(BT)W^{\perp} = \operatorname{Nul}(B^{T}); in particular W⊥W^{\perp} is a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n;
  3. W∩W⊥={0}W \cap W^{\perp} = \{\mathbf{0}\}.

(a) If 𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp}, then 𝐳⋅𝐰i=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_i = 0 because each 𝐰i∈W\mathbf{w}_i \in W. Conversely, suppose 𝐳⋅𝐰i=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_i = 0 for all ii. Every 𝐰∈W\mathbf{w} \in W has the form 𝐰=c1𝐰1+⋯+ck𝐰k\mathbf{w} = c_1\mathbf{w}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{w}_k, and by linearity (Proposition 9.6) 𝐳⋅𝐰=c1(𝐳⋅𝐰1)+⋯+ck(𝐳⋅𝐰k)=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w} = c_1(\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_1) + \cdots + c_k(\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_k) = 0.

(b) Row ii of BTB^{T} is 𝐰iT\mathbf{w}_i^{T}, so the ii-th entry of BT𝐳B^{T}\mathbf{z} is 𝐰iT𝐳=𝐰i⋅𝐳\mathbf{w}_i^{T}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{w}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}. Hence BT𝐳=0B^{T}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} if and only if 𝐳⋅𝐰i=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_i = 0 for all ii, which by (a) means 𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp}. A null space is a subspace by Theorem 6.21.

(c) If 𝐳∈W∩W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W \cap W^{\perp}, then 𝐳\mathbf{z} is orthogonal to every vector of WW, in particular to itself: 𝐳⋅𝐳=0\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{z} = 0, so 𝐳=0\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} by part (c) of Proposition 9.6.

Proposition 9.23 (The dimension of W⊥W^{\perp})¶

For every subspace WW of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, dimW+dimW⊥=nand(W⊥)⊥=W.\dim W + \dim W^{\perp} = n \qquad\text{and}\qquad (W^{\perp})^{\perp} = W .

If W={0}W = \{\mathbf{0}\}, then W⊥=ℝnW^{\perp} = \mathbb{R}^n and (W⊥)⊥={0}(W^{\perp})^{\perp} = \{\mathbf{0}\} (the only vector orthogonal to all of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n is orthogonal to itself), so both statements hold. Otherwise let 𝐰1,…,𝐰k\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k be a basis of WW, so k=dimWk = \dim W, and let B=(𝐰1⋯𝐰k)B = (\mathbf{w}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{w}_k). The kk columns of BB are independent, so rankB=k\operatorname{rank} B = k, and rankBT=rankB=k\operatorname{rank} B^{T} = \operatorname{rank} B = k by the rank theorem (Theorem 7.9). The matrix BTB^{T} is k×nk \times n, so the rank theorem gives dimNul(BT)=n−k\dim \operatorname{Nul}(B^{T}) = n - k, and Nul(BT)=W⊥\operatorname{Nul}(B^{T}) = W^{\perp} by Proposition 9.22. Hence dimW⊥=n−k\dim W^{\perp} = n - k.

For the second statement, every 𝐰∈W\mathbf{w} \in W is orthogonal to every 𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp} (this is Definition 9.21, read from the other side), so W⊆(W⊥)⊥W \subseteq (W^{\perp})^{\perp}. Applying the first statement to the subspace W⊥W^{\perp}, dim(W⊥)⊥=n−(n−k)=k=dimW\dim (W^{\perp})^{\perp} = n - (n - k) = k = \dim W. A subspace of (W⊥)⊥(W^{\perp})^{\perp} with the same dimension is all of it, by part (3) of Theorem 6.47, so W=(W⊥)⊥W = (W^{\perp})^{\perp}.

Lemma 9.27 (BTBB^{T}B is invertible)¶

Let BB be an n×kn \times k matrix whose columns are linearly independent. Then the k×kk \times k matrix BTBB^{T}B is invertible.

Let BTB𝐜=0B^{T}B\,\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}. Theorem 9.28 (The orthogonal projection theorem)¶

Let WW be a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n and 𝐛∈ℝn\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n. Then 𝐛\mathbf{b} can be written uniquely as 𝐛=𝐛̂+𝐳,𝐛̂∈W,𝐳∈W⊥.\mathbf{b} = \hat{\mathbf{b}} + \mathbf{z}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{b}} \in W, \quad \mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp} .(9.2) Moreover, if 𝐰1,…,𝐰k\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k is a basis of WW and B=(𝐰1⋯𝐰k)B = (\mathbf{w}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{w}_k), then 𝐛̂=B𝐜,where𝐜=(BTB)−1BT𝐛,\hat{\mathbf{b}} = B\mathbf{c}, \qquad\text{where}\qquad \mathbf{c} = (B^{T}B)^{-1}B^{T}\mathbf{b} , that is, 𝐜\mathbf{c} is the unique solution of the k×kk \times k system BTB𝐜=BT𝐛.B^{T}B\,\mathbf{c} = B^{T}\mathbf{b} .(9.3)

If W={0}W = \{\mathbf{0}\}, then W⊥=ℝnW^{\perp} = \mathbb{R}^n and the only decomposition is 𝐛=0+𝐛\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{b}. Step 1: what a decomposition means for coordinates. A vector 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}} lies in WW exactly when 𝐛̂=c1𝐰1+⋯+ck𝐰k=B𝐜\hat{\mathbf{b}} = c_1\mathbf{w}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{w}_k = B\mathbf{c} for some 𝐜=(c1,…,ck)T\mathbf{c} = (c_1,\, \dots,\, c_k)^{T} (the column view of B𝐜B\mathbf{c}, Theorem 1.12). 9.4  Distance to a subspace and the projection formula

💡 Intuition

The picture of the projection theorem promised more than a decomposition: the foot of the perpendicular is the point of WW closest to 𝐛\mathbf{b}. This is Pythagoras once more, and it is the fact that will solve the least-squares problem. The section then collects the algebraic properties of 𝐛↦𝐛̂\mathbf{b} \mapsto \hat{\mathbf{b}} and gives the formula that makes projections easy to compute: when the basis of WW is orthogonal, the system (9.3) is diagonal and can be solved by inspection.

Theorem 9.32 (Best approximation)¶

Let WW be a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, 𝐛∈ℝn\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n and 𝐛̂=projW𝐛\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \operatorname{proj}_W \mathbf{b}. Then for every 𝐰∈W\mathbf{w} \in W, ∥𝐛−𝐰∥2=∥𝐛−𝐛̂∥2+∥𝐛̂−𝐰∥2,\|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{w}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\|^2 + \|\hat{\mathbf{b}} - \mathbf{w}\|^2 , so ∥𝐛−𝐰∥>∥𝐛−𝐛̂∥\|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{w}\| > \|\mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\| whenever 𝐰≠𝐛̂\mathbf{w} \neq \hat{\mathbf{b}}. In words, 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}} is the unique point of WW closest to 𝐛\mathbf{b}.

Write 𝐛−𝐰=(𝐛−𝐛̂)+(𝐛̂−𝐰)\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}) + (\hat{\mathbf{b}} - \mathbf{w}). The first vector is 𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp}, and the second lies in WW, being a difference of two vectors of WW. So they are orthogonal, and Pythagoras (Theorem 9.11) gives the identity. If 𝐰≠𝐛̂\mathbf{w} \neq \hat{\mathbf{b}}, the last term is positive.

Theorem 9.34 (Properties of the projection)¶

  1. p:ℝn→ℝnp\colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n is a linear map.
  2. p(𝐛)=𝐛p(\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{b} if and only if 𝐛∈W\mathbf{b} \in W, and p(𝐛)=0p(\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{0} if and only if 𝐛∈W⊥\mathbf{b} \in W^{\perp}. So the image of pp is WW and its kernel is W⊥W^{\perp}.

  4. projW⊥𝐛=𝐛−projW𝐛\operatorname{proj}_{W^{\perp}} \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} - \operatorname{proj}_W \mathbf{b}.

Remark 9.35 (The projection as a diagonalizable map)¶

9.5  The Gram–Schmidt process and the QR factorization

💡 Intuition

The projection formula needs an orthogonal basis, and the bases we meet in practice, such as the columns of a matrix, are seldom orthogonal. The Gram–Schmidt process repairs this one vector at a time: keep the first vector; from the second subtract its projection onto the first; from the third subtract its projection onto the span of the first two, which we can now compute because those two are already orthogonal; and so on. What is subtracted at each step is a combination of earlier vectors, so the spans do not change. Recording the subtracted coefficients turns the process into a factorization A=QRA = QR, which is to orthogonality what row reduction is to solving equations.

Theorem 9.40 (The Gram–Schmidt process)¶

Let 𝐮1,…,𝐮p\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_p be linearly independent vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n. Define 𝐯1=𝐮1\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{u}_1 and, for k=2,…,pk = 2, \dots, p, 𝐯k=𝐮k−∑j=1k−1𝐮k⋅𝐯j𝐯j⋅𝐯j𝐯j,\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{u}_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{\mathbf{u}_k \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}{\mathbf{v}_j \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}\,\mathbf{v}_j ,(9.6) that is, 𝐯2=𝐮2−𝐮2⋅𝐯1𝐯1⋅𝐯1𝐯1,𝐯3=𝐮3−𝐮3⋅𝐯1𝐯1⋅𝐯1𝐯1−𝐮3⋅𝐯2𝐯2⋅𝐯2𝐯2,\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_2 &= \mathbf{u}_2 - \frac{\mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1}{\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1}\,\mathbf{v}_1, \\ \mathbf{v}_3 &= \mathbf{u}_3 - \frac{\mathbf{u}_3 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1}{\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1}\,\mathbf{v}_1 - \frac{\mathbf{u}_3 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2}{\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2}\,\mathbf{v}_2, \end{aligned} and so on. Induction on kk. For k=1k = 1: 𝐯1=𝐮1≠0\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{u}_1 \neq \mathbf{0}, since independent vectors are nonzero, and the spans agree.

Let k≥2k \ge 2 and assume the statement for k−1k - 1: the vectors 𝐯1,…,𝐯k−1\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k-1} are nonzero, pairwise orthogonal, and span Wk−1=Span(𝐮1,…,𝐮k−1)W_{k-1} = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{k-1}). Remark 9.41 (Practical points)¶

(a) Each 𝐯j\mathbf{v}_j may be replaced by any nonzero multiple c𝐯jc\,\mathbf{v}_j before it is used in later steps: the term 𝐮k⋅c𝐯jc𝐯j⋅c𝐯jc𝐯j\frac{\mathbf{u}_k \cdot c\mathbf{v}_j}{c\mathbf{v}_j \cdot c\mathbf{v}_j}\,c\,\mathbf{v}_j equals 𝐮k⋅𝐯j𝐯j⋅𝐯j𝐯j\frac{\mathbf{u}_k \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}{\mathbf{v}_j \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}\,\mathbf{v}_j. So every subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n has an orthogonal, and hence an orthonormal, basis.

Explicitly, let 𝐯1,…,𝐯n\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n be the Gram–Schmidt vectors of the columns 𝐮1,…,𝐮n\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_n of AA, and write the relations (9.7) as A=VTA = VT, where V=(𝐯1⋯𝐯n)V = (\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n) and TT is the upper triangular matrix with entries tjkt_{jk} above the diagonal and 11 on it. Remark 9.45 (Computing RR)¶

No extra work is needed to find RR: the Gram–Schmidt process already produces the relations (9.7), that is, column kk of TT, and (9.9) turns them into column kk of RR by multiplying the coefficient of 𝐯j\mathbf{v}_j by ∥𝐯j∥\|\mathbf{v}_j\|. This works just as well with rescaled vectors 𝐯j′\mathbf{v}_j' (point (a) of Remark 9.41): write each 𝐮k\mathbf{u}_k in terms of the 𝐯j′\mathbf{v}_j' and multiply the coefficient of 𝐯j′\mathbf{v}_j' by ∥𝐯j′∥\|\mathbf{v}_j'\|, because 𝐯j′=∥𝐯j′∥𝐪j\mathbf{v}_j' = \|\mathbf{v}_j'\|\,\mathbf{q}_j as well. The formula R=QTAR = Q^{T}A, whose (j,k)(j, k) entry is 𝐪j⋅𝐮k\mathbf{q}_j \cdot \mathbf{u}_k, is a good check; its entries below the diagonal come out 00. For a square AA, the matrix QQ is an orthogonal matrix, and A𝐱=Q(R𝐱)A\mathbf{x} = Q(R\mathbf{x}) writes the map 𝐱↦A𝐱\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x} as a triangular map followed by a length-preserving one. 9.6  Least-squares solutions

💡 Intuition

We return to an inconsistent system A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}. As 𝐱\mathbf{x} varies, A𝐱A\mathbf{x} runs over the subspace Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A), and the residual 𝐛−A𝐱\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} is shortest when A𝐱A\mathbf{x} is the point of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) closest to 𝐛\mathbf{b}, which is the projection 𝐛̂\hat{\mathbf{b}}. So the “best” 𝐱\mathbf{x} solves the consistent system A𝐱=𝐛̂A\mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{b}}. The three ways of computing projections in Sections 9.3–9.5 become three methods: the normal equations ATA𝐱=AT𝐛A^{T}A\,\mathbf{x} = A^{T}\mathbf{b}, the Gram–Schmidt data, and the QR factorization.

Definition 9.48 (Least-squares solution)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n and 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m. A least-squares solution of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is a vector 𝐱̂∈ℝn\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n such that ∥𝐛−A𝐱̂∥≤∥𝐛−A𝐱∥for every 𝐱∈ℝn.\|\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}\| \le \|\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}\| \qquad\text{for every } \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n . The number ∥𝐛−A𝐱̂∥\|\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}\| is the least-squares error. The name refers to ∥𝐛−A𝐱∥2=∑i(bi−(A𝐱)i)2\|\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \sum_i (b_i - (A\mathbf{x})_i)^2: a least-squares solution makes the sum of the squares of the mm errors as small as possible. If A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is consistent, its least-squares solutions are its ordinary solutions, with error 00.

Theorem 9.49 (Characterization of least-squares solutions)¶

Let AA be m×nm \times n, 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m, and 𝐛̂=projCol(A)𝐛\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}\mathbf{b}. For 𝐱̂∈ℝn\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n the following are equivalent:

  1. 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} is a least-squares solution of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b};
  2. A𝐱̂=𝐛̂A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{b}};
  3. 𝐛−A𝐱̂\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} is orthogonal to every column of AA;
  4. 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} solves the normal equations ATA𝐱=AT𝐛.A^{T}A\,\mathbf{x} = A^{T}\mathbf{b} .(9.10)
Least-squares solutions always exist, and the least-squares error is dist(𝐛,Col(A))=∥𝐛−𝐛̂∥\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{b}, \operatorname{Col}(A)) = \|\mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\|.

Let W=Col(A)W = \operatorname{Col}(A); the vectors A𝐱A\mathbf{x}, for 𝐱∈ℝn\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n, are exactly the vectors of WW.

(a) ⇔\Leftrightarrow (b). By Theorem 9.32, ∥𝐛−𝐰∥\|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{w}\| for 𝐰∈W\mathbf{w} \in W is smallest exactly at 𝐰=𝐛̂\mathbf{w} = \hat{\mathbf{b}}. So 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} minimizes ∥𝐛−A𝐱∥\|\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}\| if and only if A𝐱̂=𝐛̂A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{b}}.

(b) ⇔\Leftrightarrow (c). If A𝐱̂=𝐛̂A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{b}}, then 𝐛−A𝐱̂=𝐳∈W⊥\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp}. Conversely, if 𝐛−A𝐱̂∈W⊥\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in W^{\perp} (by part (a) of Proposition 9.22 this is (c)), then 𝐛=A𝐱̂+(𝐛−A𝐱̂)\mathbf{b} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} + (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) is a decomposition with parts in WW and W⊥W^{\perp}, and by uniqueness in the projection theorem (Theorem 9.28), A𝐱̂=𝐛̂A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{b}}.

(c) ⇔\Leftrightarrow (d). The jj-th entry of AT(𝐛−A𝐱̂)A^{T}(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) is the inner product of column jj of AA with 𝐛−A𝐱̂\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}. So (c) says AT(𝐛−A𝐱̂)=0A^{T}(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}, which is (d).

Finally, 𝐛̂∈W=Col(A)\hat{\mathbf{b}} \in W = \operatorname{Col}(A), so A𝐱=𝐛̂A\mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{b}} is consistent, and its solutions are least-squares solutions by (b). The error is ∥𝐛−𝐛̂∥\|\mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\|.

Theorem 9.50 (When the least-squares solution is unique)¶

For an m×nm \times n matrix AA the following are equivalent:

  1. the columns of AA are linearly independent;
  2. ATAA^{T}A is invertible;
  3. for every 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m, the system A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} has exactly one least-squares solution.
In that case 𝐱̂=(ATA)−1AT𝐛.\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}\mathbf{b} .(9.11) If A=QRA = QR is a QR factorization, then 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} is the unique solution of the triangular system R𝐱=QT𝐛,R\,\mathbf{x} = Q^{T}\mathbf{b},(9.12) that is, 𝐱̂=R−1QT𝐛\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^{-1}Q^{T}\mathbf{b}, and 𝐛̂=QQT𝐛\hat{\mathbf{b}} = QQ^{T}\mathbf{b}.

(a) ⇒\Rightarrow (b) is Lemma 9.27. (b) ⇒\Rightarrow (a): by Remark 9.30, Nul(A)=Nul(ATA)\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \operatorname{Nul}(A^{T}A), which is {0}\{\mathbf{0}\} when ATAA^{T}A is invertible, so the columns of AA are independent. (c) ⇒\Rightarrow (a): take 𝐛=0\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}, whose least-squares solutions are the solutions of A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}; if they are unique, the columns are independent.

Remark 9.52 (Three methods)¶

For a matrix AA with independent columns we now have three ways to the least-squares solution: (a) the Gram–Schmidt data, with Proposition 9.51; (b) the QR factorization, solving (9.12); (c) the normal equations (9.10). By hand, (c) is usually the shortest for small matrices. In floating-point arithmetic, (b) is the standard choice: forming ATAA^{T}A squares the sensitivity of the problem to rounding errors, while QQ preserves lengths (Proposition 9.19) and so does not magnify errors. Library routines for least squares (such as the backslash operator of MATLAB, or numpy.linalg.lstsq) are based on orthogonal factorizations, not on the normal equations.

Remark 9.58 (Looking ahead)¶

Least squares fits not only lines: any model that is linear in its unknown coefficients, such as a parabola c0+c1t+c2t2c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 or a combination of sines and cosines, leads to an inconsistent system and to the same normal equations.

When the columns of AA are dependent, the least-squares solutions form the set 𝐱̂+Nul(A)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \operatorname{Nul}(A) (Remark 9.57), and the one of smallest length is the unique least-squares solution lying in Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A), the orthogonal complement of Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A). It is written 𝐱+=A+𝐛\mathbf{x}^{+} = A^{+}\mathbf{b}, where A+A^{+} is the pseudoinverse of AA; when the columns are independent, A+=(ATA)−1ATA^{+} = (A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T} and 𝐱+\mathbf{x}^{+} is the 𝐱̂\hat{\mathbf{x}} of (9.11). Both A+A^{+} and the most reliable numerical least-squares methods come from the singular value decomposition (SVD) A=UΣVTA = U\Sigma V^{T}, which exists for every m×nm \times n matrix: UU (m×mm \times m) and VV (n×nn \times n) are orthogonal matrices, and Σ\Sigma is an m×nm \times n matrix whose only nonzero entries are the singular values σ1≥⋯≥σr>0\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0 in positions (1,1),…,(r,r)(1,1), \dots, (r,r), with r=rankAr = \operatorname{rank} A. Then A+=VΣ+UTA^{+} = V\Sigma^{+}U^{T}, where the n×mn \times m matrix Σ+\Sigma^{+} has 1/σ1,…,1/σr1/\sigma_1, \dots, 1/\sigma_r in the same positions. The SVD also shows how close AA is to having dependent columns, which is what matters when the data are rounded.

The matrices of this lecture also carry a structural message. A projection matrix satisfies PT=PP^{T} = P and is diagonalizable with an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors, and ATAA^{T}A is symmetric as well. The spectral theorem says that this is true of every symmetric matrix: it can be diagonalized as A=QDQTA = QDQ^{T} with QQ orthogonal, which removes both obstacles to diagonalization met in Lecture 8. Applied to the symmetric matrix ATAA^{T}A, whose eigenvalues are σ12,…,σr2\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2 and 00, it gives the matrix VV and the singular values of the SVD.
