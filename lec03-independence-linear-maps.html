3.1  Homogeneous systems and the structure of solution sets

💡 Intuition

Among all right-hand sides, one is special: 𝐛=𝟎\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}. A system with zero right-hand side can never be inconsistent — the zero vector always solves it — so the interesting question flips from whether solutions exist to how many. And, as we are about to prove, whoever understands A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} understands every A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}: the two solution sets differ only by a shift.

Definition 3.1 (Homogeneous system)¶

A linear system of the form A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} is called homogeneous. It is always consistent, since 𝐱=𝟎\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} is a solution — the trivial solution. The interesting question is whether a nonzero solution exists.

Example 3.2 (Solving a homogeneous system)¶

Let A=(1203241836111)with columns 𝐚1,𝐚2,𝐚3,𝐚4.A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 11 \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{with columns } \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3, \mathbf{a}_4 . Row reduction (R2−2R1R_2 - 2R_1, R3−3R1R_3 - 3R_1, then R3−R2R_3 - R_2) brings the augmented matrix of A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} to reduced echelon form: (1203024180361110)→R2−2R1R3−3R1R3−R2(120300012000000)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 8 & 0 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 11 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\substack{R_2 - 2R_1 \\ R_3 - 3R_1 \\ R_3 - R_2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} Pivots in columns 11 and 33; the variables x2=sx_2 = s and x4=tx_4 = t are free. The parametric vector form of the solutions of A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} is 𝐱=s(−2100)+t(−30−21),s,t∈ℝ\mathbf{x} = s \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad s, t \in \mathbb{R} — a plane through the origin inside ℝ4\mathbb{R}^4, with one direction vector per free variable. Note there is no ``particular solution'' vector out front: for a homogeneous system the trivial solution plays that role. Keep this example; it works overtime in this lecture.

Theorem 3.3 (Solution sets of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} and A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0})¶

Suppose A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is consistent and let 𝐩\mathbf{p} be one particular solution. Then the solution set of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is exactly {𝐩+𝐡:𝐡 is a solution of A𝐱=𝟎}.\{\, \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h} \;:\; \mathbf{h} \text{ is a solution of } A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \,\}. In words: the solution set of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is the solution set of the homogeneous system translated by 𝐩\mathbf{p}.

Both directions come from linearity. If A𝐡=𝟎A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}, then A(𝐩+𝐡)=A𝐩+A𝐡=𝐛+𝟎=𝐛A(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h}) = A\mathbf{p} + A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{b}, so 𝐩+𝐡\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h} is a solution. Conversely, if A𝐰=𝐛A\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{b}, set 𝐡=𝐰−𝐩\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{p}; then A𝐡=A𝐰−A𝐩=𝐛−𝐛=𝟎A\mathbf{h} = A\mathbf{w} - A\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}, so 𝐰=𝐩+𝐡\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h} with 𝐡\mathbf{h} a homogeneous solution.

□
Example 3.4 (Translation in action)¶

Continue with the matrix AA of the homogeneous example, and take 𝐛=𝐚1+𝐚3=(134)T\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}^{T}. By the column view, 𝐩=(1010)T\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^{T} is a particular solution of A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}, and by the structure theorem the complete solution set is 𝐱=(1010)+s(−2100)+t(−30−21),s,t∈ℝ\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + s \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad s, t \in \mathbb{R} — the same plane of homogeneous solutions, shifted by 𝐩\mathbf{p}. Compare with Lecture 2's parametric example: the “particular solution plus direction vectors” shape we computed there was not an accident; it is the structure theorem in action.

3.2  Linear independence

💡 Intuition

When does a collection of vectors contain redundancy — one vector expressible from the others? The column view turns this qualitative question into a computation: pack the vectors as the columns of a matrix AA; redundancy is exactly a nonzero solution of A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}. The homogeneous machinery of the last section thus becomes a redundancy detector.

Definition 3.5 (Linear independence)¶

Vectors 𝐯1,…,𝐯p∈ℝm\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p \in \mathbb{R}^m are linearly independent if the only weights with x1𝐯1+x2𝐯2+⋯+xp𝐯p=𝟎x_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + x_p \mathbf{v}_p = \mathbf{0} are x1=x2=⋯=xp=0x_1 = x_2 = \cdots = x_p = 0. They are linearly dependent if such a combination exists with at least one weight nonzero.

Proposition 3.6 (Independence as a homogeneous system)¶

Let A=(𝐯1𝐯2⋯𝐯p)A = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_p \end{pmatrix} be the m×pm \times p matrix with the given vectors as columns. Then 𝐯1,…,𝐯p\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p are linearly dependent if and only if A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} has a nonzero solution.

By the column view, A𝐱=x1𝐯1+⋯+xp𝐯pA\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + x_p\mathbf{v}_p. So a nonzero solution of A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} is a dependence relation with some weight nonzero, and conversely.

□
Example 3.7 (Reading dependence off the null space)¶

Return to the homogeneous example. Each direction vector of the solution set is, by the proposition, a dependence relation among the columns of AA. Taking s=1,t=0s = 1, t = 0: −2𝐚1+𝐚2=𝟎-2\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2 = \mathbf{0}, i.e. 𝐚2=2𝐚1\mathbf{a}_2 = 2\mathbf{a}_1. Taking s=0,t=1s = 0, t = 1: −3𝐚1−2𝐚3+𝐚4=𝟎-3\mathbf{a}_1 - 2\mathbf{a}_3 + \mathbf{a}_4 = \mathbf{0}, i.e. 𝐚4=3𝐚1+2𝐚3\mathbf{a}_4 = 3\mathbf{a}_1 + 2\mathbf{a}_3. This is the general pattern: the parametric vector form of A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} shows exactly how each column of AA without a pivot is a linear combination of the pivot columns — one direction vector per non-pivot column, with the dependence weights visible in its entries.

Theorem 3.8 (Wide matrices have nonzero null solutions)¶

Let AA be an m×nm \times n matrix. If n>mn > m (more columns than rows), then A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} has a nonzero solution.

Bring the augmented matrix (A∣𝟎)(\,A \mid \mathbf{0}\,) to echelon form. Each pivot occupies its own row, so there are at most mm pivots. By part (3) of the existence–uniqueness theorem, the number of free variables is nn minus the number of pivots, which is at least n−m>0n - m > 0: a free variable exists. The system is consistent (it is homogeneous), so setting that free variable equal to 11 and solving produces a solution with a nonzero entry.

□
Corollary 3.9 (Too many vectors are dependent)¶

Any pp vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n with p>np > n are linearly dependent. In particular, any mm vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n are dependent whenever m>nm > n.

Pack the vectors as the columns of an n×pn \times p matrix AA. Since p>np > n, the theorem gives a nonzero solution of A𝐱=𝟎A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}, which by the proposition is a dependence relation.

□

3.3  Linear maps and their matrices

💡 Intuition

A matrix does not just store a system — it acts: feed it a vector 𝐱\mathbf{x}, receive a vector A𝐱A\mathbf{x}. Machines of this kind — functions sending vectors to vectors while respecting vector arithmetic — are everywhere in engineering: rotations, reflections, projections, coordinate changes, linear filters. This section defines them, and then proves something remarkable: they are exactly the matrices, no more and no less.

Definition 3.10 (Linear map)¶

A function T:ℝn→ℝmT\colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m is a linear map (or linear transformation) if for all 𝐮,𝐯∈ℝn\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n and all scalars c∈ℝc \in \mathbb{R}: T(𝐮+𝐯)=T(𝐮)+T(𝐯)andT(c𝐮)=cT(𝐮).T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}) \qquad\text{and}\qquad T(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,T(\mathbf{u}). Equivalently: TT turns linear combinations of inputs into the same linear combinations of outputs.

Example 3.11 (Every matrix gives a linear map)¶

For any m×nm \times n matrix AA, the function T(𝐱)=A𝐱T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} is a linear map from ℝn\mathbb{R}^n to ℝm\mathbb{R}^m: the two requirements are precisely the linearity theorem of Lecture 1. So every matrix defines a linear map. The rest of this section shows the converse.

Example 3.12 (Rotation of the plane)¶

Fix an angle θ\theta and let T:ℝ2→ℝ2T\colon \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2 rotate every vector counterclockwise by θ\theta about the origin. This is a linear map, for geometric reasons: rotating a parallelogram rotates its diagonal, so T(𝐮+𝐯)=T(𝐮)+T(𝐯)T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}); and rotating a stretched arrow is the same as stretching the rotated arrow, so T(c𝐮)=cT(𝐮)T(c\mathbf{u}) = c\,T(\mathbf{u}). (An algebraic proof will be immediate once we know its matrix.)

Example 3.13 (Flipping around a line in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3)¶

Let T:ℝ3→ℝ3T\colon \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3 flip space around the zz-axis — that is, rotate by 180∘180^\circ about the line of all (00z)T\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & z \end{pmatrix}^{T}. A point's zz-coordinate is untouched while its (x,y)(x,y)-part is sent to the opposite side: T(xyz)=(−x−yz).T\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -x \\ -y \\ z \end{pmatrix}. From this formula, linearity is an entrywise check: negating and copying entries both respect addition and scaling. Flips around other lines are also linear maps; this one has the friendliest formula because its line is a coordinate axis.

Definition 3.14 (Standard basis vectors)¶

The standard basis vectors of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n are the columns 𝐞1,…,𝐞n\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n of zeros with a single 11: the jj-th entry of 𝐞j\mathbf{e}_j is 11 and all other entries are 00. Every vector decomposes as 𝐱=x1𝐞1+x2𝐞2+⋯+xn𝐞n\mathbf{x} = x_1 \mathbf{e}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n \mathbf{e}_n — read the entries as weights.

Theorem 3.15 (The matrix of a linear map)¶

Let T:ℝn→ℝmT\colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m be a linear map. Then there is exactly one m×nm \times n matrix AA with T(𝐱)=A𝐱for all 𝐱∈ℝn,T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} \qquad\text{for all } \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n, namely the matrix whose jj-th column is T(𝐞j)T(\mathbf{e}_j): A=(T(𝐞1)T(𝐞2)⋯T(𝐞n)).A = \begin{pmatrix} T(\mathbf{e}_1) & T(\mathbf{e}_2) & \cdots & T(\mathbf{e}_n) \end{pmatrix}. AA is called the matrix of TT (or the standard matrix).

Existence. Let AA be the matrix displayed above and take any 𝐱∈ℝn\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n. Decompose 𝐱=x1𝐞1+⋯+xn𝐞n\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{e}_n as in the definition. Applying TT and using linearity repeatedly (first on the sum, then on each scalar multiple), T(𝐱)=x1T(𝐞1)+x2T(𝐞2)+⋯+xnT(𝐞n),T(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 T(\mathbf{e}_1) + x_2 T(\mathbf{e}_2) + \cdots + x_n T(\mathbf{e}_n), and by the column view this linear combination of the columns of AA with weights x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_n is exactly A𝐱A\mathbf{x}.

Uniqueness. Suppose A𝐱=B𝐱A\mathbf{x} = B\mathbf{x} for all 𝐱\mathbf{x}. Taking 𝐱=𝐞j\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_j picks out the jj-th columns: A𝐞j=B𝐞jA\mathbf{e}_j = B\mathbf{e}_j says the jj-th columns of AA and BB agree, for every jj. Hence A=BA = B: no other matrix represents TT.

□
Corollary 3.16 (Matrices ↔\leftrightarrow linear maps)¶

Every m×nm \times n matrix defines a linear map ℝn→ℝm\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m (via 𝐱↦A𝐱\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}), and every linear map ℝn→ℝm\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m is defined by exactly one m×nm \times n matrix (the theorem). So there is a one-to-one correspondence {m×n matrices}↔{linear maps ℝn→ℝm}.\{\, m \times n \text{ matrices} \,\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \{\, \text{linear maps } \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \,\}. Matrices and linear maps are two descriptions of the same objects: the matrix is the data, the map is the action. To find the matrix of any linear map, feed it the standard basis vectors and record the outputs as columns.

Example 3.17 (The rotation matrix)¶

Apply the recipe to rotation by θ\theta. Rotating 𝐞1\mathbf{e}_1 (which points along the positive xx-axis) by θ\theta lands on (cosθsinθ)T\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \end{pmatrix}^{T}; rotating 𝐞2\mathbf{e}_2 lands on (−sinθcosθ)T\begin{pmatrix} -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}^{T}. By the theorem, the matrix of the rotation is Rθ=(cosθ−sinθsinθcosθ),R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, and rotation by θ\theta is simply 𝐱↦Rθ𝐱\mathbf{x} \mapsto R_\theta \mathbf{x} — trigonometry packed into four entries. (This also delivers the promised algebraic proof that rotation is linear: it is a matrix map.)

Example 3.18 (The flip matrix)¶

For the flip around the zz-axis: T(𝐞1)=−𝐞1T(\mathbf{e}_1) = -\mathbf{e}_1, T(𝐞2)=−𝐞2T(\mathbf{e}_2) = -\mathbf{e}_2, and T(𝐞3)=𝐞3T(\mathbf{e}_3) = \mathbf{e}_3. So its matrix is F=(−1000−10001),F = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, and indeed F(xyz)T=(−x−yz)TF \begin{pmatrix} x & y & z \end{pmatrix}^{T} = \begin{pmatrix} -x & -y & z \end{pmatrix}^{T}.

Remark 3.19 (Looking ahead)¶

The correspondence between matrices and linear maps is the conceptual heart of the course. From now on, every question has two faces: an algebraic one about a matrix, and a geometric one about a map. Next week we exploit this dictionary: composing maps will give us matrix multiplication, and undoing a map will give us the inverse matrix.
