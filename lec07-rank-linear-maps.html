7.1  Bases for the null space, column space, and row space

💡 Intuition

Lecture 6 attached three subspaces to every matrix. One row reduction produces a basis of each of them, and the recipe is short: the pivot columns of AA for the column space, the nonzero rows of an echelon form for the row space, and the vectors of the parametric vector form for the null space. The one subtle point is that row operations change the column space but not the relations among the columns; that is why the basis of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) is taken from AA itself.

Lemma 7.1 (Row operations keep column relations)¶

Let BB be obtained from the m×nm \times n matrix AA by a sequence of row operations. Then Nul(B)=Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(B) = \operatorname{Nul}(A). Consequently, for all scalars x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_n, x1𝐚1+⋯+xn𝐚n=0⇔x1𝐛1+⋯+xn𝐛n=0,x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_1\mathbf{b}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{b}_n = \mathbf{0}, where 𝐚j\mathbf{a}_j and 𝐛j\mathbf{b}_j are the columns of AA and BB. In particular a set of columns of AA is independent if and only if the corresponding columns of BB are, and 𝐚j\mathbf{a}_j is a combination of certain other columns of AA, with certain weights, if and only if 𝐛j\mathbf{b}_j is the same combination of the corresponding columns of BB.

By Lecture 4, B=EAB = EA where EE is a product of elementary matrices, which is invertible by the lemma on elementary matrices. If A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} then B𝐱=EA𝐱=0B\mathbf{x} = EA\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}, and if B𝐱=0B\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} then A𝐱=E−1B𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = E^{-1}B\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}. By the column view the two displayed equations say A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} and B𝐱=0B\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}.

□
Theorem 7.2 (A basis of the column space)¶

The pivot columns of AA form a basis of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A).

Let RR be the reduced echelon form of AA, with rr pivots. Its pivot columns are 𝐞1,…,𝐞r∈ℝm\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_r \in \mathbb{R}^m (each pivot is 11 and the only nonzero entry of its column), so they are independent. Every other column of RR has zeros in rows r+1,…,mr + 1, \dots, m, which are zero rows, so it is a combination of 𝐞1,…,𝐞r\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_r. By the lemma, the same two statements hold for the corresponding columns of AA: the pivot columns of AA are independent, and every other column of AA is a combination of them. Removing the other columns one at a time does not change the span, by Lecture 6, so the pivot columns of AA span Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A).

□
Remark 7.3 (Take the columns from AA, not from its echelon form)¶

Row operations usually change the column space. In Lecture 6's example, every vector of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) satisfies b3=b1+b2b_3 = b_1 + b_2, while every column of the reduced echelon form has third entry 00; the two column spaces are different planes in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3. The echelon form tells us which columns to take; the columns themselves come from AA.

Lemma 7.4 (Row operations keep the row space)¶

If BB is obtained from AA by row operations, then Row(B)=Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(B) = \operatorname{Row}(A).

This is the remark of Lecture 6 on row operations: a replacement leaves the span of the rows unchanged by the proposition on replacing a vector, a swap only reorders the rows, and a scaling by c≠0c \neq 0 can be undone by scaling by 1/c1/c.

□
Theorem 7.5 (A basis of the row space)¶

The nonzero rows of any echelon form UU of AA (written as columns) form a basis of Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A).

By the lemma, Row(A)=Row(U)\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Row}(U), which is spanned by the nonzero rows 𝐮1,…,𝐮r\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_r of UU (zero rows add nothing). To see that they are independent, suppose c1𝐮1+⋯+cr𝐮r=0c_1\mathbf{u}_1 + \cdots + c_r\mathbf{u}_r = \mathbf{0}. In the column of the pivot of 𝐮1\mathbf{u}_1, the rows below have zeros (the staircase shape), so that entry of the combination is c1c_1 times the pivot; hence c1=0c_1 = 0. Then look at the pivot column of 𝐮2\mathbf{u}_2, whose entries in rows 3,…,r3, \dots, r are zero, to get c2=0c_2 = 0; and so on, down to cr=0c_r = 0.

□
Theorem 7.6 (A basis of the null space)¶

Solve A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} by row reduction and write the solution in parametric vector form, with one vector for each free variable. These vectors form a basis of Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A). In particular dimNul(A)\dim \operatorname{Nul}(A) is the number of free variables, and Nul(A)={0}\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\} when there are none.

The vectors span Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) by construction. The vector belonging to the free variable xjx_j has entry 11 in position jj, and the vectors belonging to the other free variables have entry 00 in position jj. So in a combination equal to 0\mathbf{0}, entry jj shows that the weight of the vector of xjx_j is 00, for every free variable xjx_j.

7.2  Rank and the rank theorem

💡 Intuition

The example just computed shows a pattern: three pivots, a column space of dimension 33, a row space of dimension 33, and a null space of dimension 5−3=25 - 3 = 2. All three dimensions are read off the single number of pivots. This is the rank theorem, and it says something that is not at all obvious from the definitions: the columns and the rows of any matrix span spaces of the same dimension, even though these spaces live in different ℝm\mathbb{R}^m and ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

Definition 7.8 (Rank)¶

The rank of a matrix AA is the dimension of its column space: rankA=dimCol(A)\operatorname{rank} A = \dim \operatorname{Col}(A). The dimension of Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) is sometimes called the nullity of AA.

Theorem 7.9 (The rank theorem)¶

Let AA be an m×nm \times n matrix whose echelon form has rr pivots. Then rankA=dimCol(A)=dimRow(A)=r,dimNul(A)=n−r.\operatorname{rank} A = \dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = \dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = r, \qquad \dim \operatorname{Nul}(A) = n - r . Consequently rankA+dimNul(A)=n(the number of columns),\operatorname{rank} A + \dim \operatorname{Nul}(A) = n \quad (\text{the number of columns}), rankAT=rankA\operatorname{rank} A^{T} = \operatorname{rank} A, and rankA≤min(m,n)\operatorname{rank} A \le \min(m, n).

By the column-space basis there are rr pivot columns in a basis of Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A); by the row-space basis there are rr nonzero rows in a basis of Row(A)\operatorname{Row}(A); and by the null-space basis together with the counting rule of Lecture 2, a basis of Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) has one vector per free variable, that is, n−rn - r vectors. Since Col(AT)=Row(A)\operatorname{Col}(A^{T}) = \operatorname{Row}(A), we get rankAT=dimRow(A)=r\operatorname{rank} A^{T} = \dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = r. Finally, each pivot uses its own row and its own column, so r≤mr \le m and r≤nr \le n.

□
Example 7.10 (The rank theorem at work)¶

For the 4×54 \times 5 matrix of the three-bases example, rankA=3\operatorname{rank} A = 3 and dimNul(A)=2\dim \operatorname{Nul}(A) = 2, and 3+2=53 + 2 = 5.

The theorem also answers questions without any computation.

  • A 6×96 \times 9 matrix AA has a null space of dimension 33. Then rankA=9−3=6\operatorname{rank} A = 9 - 3 = 6, so Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A) is a 66-dimensional subspace of ℝ6\mathbb{R}^6, which is all of ℝ6\mathbb{R}^6 by Lecture 6: the system A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is consistent for every 𝐛∈ℝ6\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^6.
  • A 5×75 \times 7 matrix has rank at most 55, so its null space has dimension at least 7−5=27 - 5 = 2; it can never be a line.
  • A 7×57 \times 5 matrix has rank at most 55, so its columns span at most a 55-dimensional subspace of ℝ7\mathbb{R}^7, and some systems A𝐱=𝐛A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} are inconsistent.

Theorem 7.11 (The reduced echelon form is unique)¶

Every matrix AA has exactly one reduced row echelon form.

Let RR and SS be reduced echelon forms of AA. By the lemma on column relations, applied to A→RA \to R and to A→SA \to S, a linear relation holds among columns of RR if and only if it holds among the same columns of AA, if and only if it holds among the same columns of SS.

In a reduced echelon form, column jj is a pivot column exactly when it is not a combination of the columns before it: a pivot column with its pivot in row kk has a 11 in row kk, while all earlier columns are zero in rows k,…,mk, \dots, m; and a non-pivot column is a combination of the pivot columns before it, as in the proof of the column-space theorem. This property is a statement about column relations, so RR and SS have the same pivot columns j1<⋯<jrj_1 < \cdots < j_r, which in both matrices are 𝐞1,…,𝐞r\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_r, in this order.

A non-pivot column jj of RR is ∑krkj𝐞k=∑krkj(column jk of R)\sum_k r_{kj}\mathbf{e}_k = \sum_k r_{kj}\,(\text{column } j_k \text{ of } R). The same relation then holds in AA, so 𝐚j=∑krkj𝐚jk\mathbf{a}_j = \sum_k r_{kj}\mathbf{a}_{j_k}, and likewise 𝐚j=∑kskj𝐚jk\mathbf{a}_j = \sum_k s_{kj}\mathbf{a}_{j_k}. The pivot columns of AA are independent, so by unique representation rkj=skjr_{kj} = s_{kj} for all kk. Hence every column of RR equals the corresponding column of SS, and R=SR = S.

□
Remark 7.12 (A promise kept)¶

Lecture 2 took the uniqueness of the reduced echelon form on faith, which is why the number of pivots, and hence the rank, is a property of AA and not of the way we reduce it. The theorem proves it, and the rank theorem gives a second reason: the number of pivots of any echelon form equals dimCol(A)\dim \operatorname{Col}(A).

Theorem 7.13 (The invertibility theorem, continued)¶

For an n×nn \times n matrix AA the following are equivalent to the invertibility of AA, and so to every condition of Lecture 4's theorem and to detA≠0\det A \neq 0:

  1. rankA=n\operatorname{rank} A = n;
  2. Nul(A)={0}\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\};
  3. Col(A)=ℝn\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n;
  4. Row(A)=ℝn\operatorname{Row}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n;
  5. the columns of AA form a basis of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

By the rank theorem, each of (1)–(4) says that an echelon form of AA has nn pivots (for (3) and (4), use part (3) of Lecture 6's theorem: an nn-dimensional subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n is ℝn\mathbb{R}^n), which is condition (3) of the invertibility theorem. Condition (5) is Lecture 6's theorem on bases of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

□

7.3  Linear maps between vector spaces

💡 Intuition

Lecture 3 defined linear maps from ℝn\mathbb{R}^n to ℝm\mathbb{R}^m. The definition makes sense between any two vector spaces, and many familiar operations turn out to be linear: differentiating a polynomial, transposing a matrix, evaluating a function at a point. The same questions apply to all of them (which vectors go to 0\mathbf{0}, which outputs are reached), and the rank theorem has a version for maps. A linear map is also economical to describe: its values on a basis determine it completely.

Definition 7.14 (Linear map)¶

Let VV and WW be vector spaces. A function f:V→Wf\colon V \to W is a linear map (or linear transformation) if for all 𝐮,𝐯∈V\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V and c∈ℝc \in \mathbb{R}, f(𝐮+𝐯)=f(𝐮)+f(𝐯)andf(c𝐮)=cf(𝐮).f(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = f(\mathbf{u}) + f(\mathbf{v}) \qquad\text{and}\qquad f(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,f(\mathbf{u}) . As in Lecture 3, it follows that f(c1𝐯1+⋯+ck𝐯k)=c1f(𝐯1)+⋯+ckf(𝐯k)f(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k) = c_1 f(\mathbf{v}_1) + \cdots + c_k f(\mathbf{v}_k), and f(0)=f(00)=0f(0)=0f(\mathbf{0}) = f(0\,\mathbf{0}) = 0\,f(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}.

Example 7.15 (Linear maps beyond ℝn\mathbb{R}^n)¶
  1. Differentiation D:ℙn→ℙn−1D\colon \mathbb{P}_n \to \mathbb{P}_{n-1}, D(p)=p′D(p) = p', is linear: (p+q)′=p′+q′(p + q)' = p' + q' and (cp)′=cp′(c\,p)' = c\,p', the sum and constant-multiple rules of calculus. For example D(2−t+4t2+t3)=−1+8t+3t2D(2 - t + 4t^2 + t^3) = -1 + 8t + 3t^2.
  2. Transpose ℝm×n→ℝn×m\mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \to \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, A↦ATA \mapsto A^{T}, is linear: (A+B)T=AT+BT(A + B)^{T} = A^{T} + B^{T} and (cA)T=cAT(cA)^{T} = cA^{T}.
  3. Evaluation ev:ℙ3→ℝ3\operatorname{ev}\colon \mathbb{P}_3 \to \mathbb{R}^3, ev(p)=(p(0),p(1),p(2))T\operatorname{ev}(p) = (p(0),\, p(1),\, p(2))^{T}, is linear, because (p+q)(t)=p(t)+q(t)(p + q)(t) = p(t) + q(t) and (cp)(t)=cp(t)(c\,p)(t) = c\,p(t) at each tt.
  4. Coordinates. For a basis ℬ\mathcal{B} of VV with nn vectors, 𝐯↦[𝐯]ℬ\mathbf{v} \mapsto [\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} is a linear map V→ℝnV \to \mathbb{R}^n, by Lecture 6.
Two non-examples: det:ℝ2×2→ℝ\det\colon \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2} \to \mathbb{R} is not linear, since det(2I2)=4≠2detI2\det(2I_2) = 4 \neq 2\det I_2; and p↦p+1p \mapsto p + 1 is not linear, since it sends 00 to 11.
Definition 7.16 (Kernel and image)¶

For a linear map f:V→Wf\colon V \to W, the kernel and the image of ff are kerf={𝐯∈V:f(𝐯)=0},imf={f(𝐯):𝐯∈V}.\ker f = \{\, \mathbf{v} \in V : f(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0} \,\}, \qquad \operatorname{im} f = \{\, f(\mathbf{v}) : \mathbf{v} \in V \,\}. For f(𝐱)=A𝐱f(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} these are Nul(A)\operatorname{Nul}(A) and Col(A)\operatorname{Col}(A), as in Lecture 6.

Proposition 7.17 (Kernel, image, and injectivity)¶

kerf\ker f is a subspace of VV, imf\operatorname{im} f is a subspace of WW, and ff is injective if and only if kerf={0}\ker f = \{\mathbf{0}\}.

f(0)=0f(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0} puts 0\mathbf{0} in both. If f(𝐮)=f(𝐯)=0f(\mathbf{u}) = f(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}, then f(𝐮+𝐯)=0f(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0} and f(c𝐮)=0f(c\,\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}. For the image, f(𝐮)+f(𝐯)=f(𝐮+𝐯)f(\mathbf{u}) + f(\mathbf{v}) = f(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) and cf(𝐮)=f(c𝐮)c\,f(\mathbf{u}) = f(c\,\mathbf{u}) are again outputs. Finally, if ff is injective and f(𝐯)=0=f(0)f(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0} = f(\mathbf{0}), then 𝐯=0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}; conversely, if kerf={0}\ker f = \{\mathbf{0}\} and f(𝐮)=f(𝐯)f(\mathbf{u}) = f(\mathbf{v}), then f(𝐮−𝐯)=0f(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}, so 𝐮=𝐯\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}.

□
Theorem 7.18 (The rank theorem for linear maps)¶

Let VV be finite-dimensional and f:V→Wf\colon V \to W linear. Then dimkerf+dimimf=dimV.\dim \ker f + \dim \operatorname{im} f = \dim V .

Choose a basis 𝐮1,…,𝐮k\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k of kerf\ker f and extend it to a basis 𝐮1,…,𝐮k,𝐯1,…,𝐯r\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k, \mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r of VV, by Lecture 6; so dimV=k+r\dim V = k + r. We show that f(𝐯1),…,f(𝐯r)f(\mathbf{v}_1), \dots, f(\mathbf{v}_r) is a basis of imf\operatorname{im} f, which gives dimimf=r\dim \operatorname{im} f = r.

Spanning. Every 𝐯∈V\mathbf{v} \in V can be written uniquely as 𝐯=a1𝐮1+⋯+ak𝐮k+b1𝐯1+⋯+br𝐯r,\mathbf{v} = a_1\mathbf{u}_1 + \cdots + a_k\mathbf{u}_k + b_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + b_r\mathbf{v}_r , by unique representation. Since ff is linear and f(𝐮i)=0f(\mathbf{u}_i) = \mathbf{0} for every ii, f(𝐯)=a1f(𝐮1)+⋯+akf(𝐮k)+b1f(𝐯1)+⋯+brf(𝐯r)=b1f(𝐯1)+⋯+brf(𝐯r).\begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{v}) &= a_1 f(\mathbf{u}_1) + \cdots + a_k f(\mathbf{u}_k) + b_1 f(\mathbf{v}_1) + \cdots + b_r f(\mathbf{v}_r) \\ &= b_1 f(\mathbf{v}_1) + \cdots + b_r f(\mathbf{v}_r) . \end{aligned} So every output of ff is a combination of f(𝐯1),…,f(𝐯r)f(\mathbf{v}_1), \dots, f(\mathbf{v}_r).

Independence. If ∑bjf(𝐯j)=0\sum b_j f(\mathbf{v}_j) = \mathbf{0}, then f(∑bj𝐯j)=0f\bigl(\sum b_j\mathbf{v}_j\bigr) = \mathbf{0}, so ∑bj𝐯j∈kerf\sum b_j\mathbf{v}_j \in \ker f and ∑bj𝐯j=∑ai𝐮i\sum b_j\mathbf{v}_j = \sum a_i\mathbf{u}_i for some aia_i. Then ∑ai𝐮i−∑bj𝐯j=0\sum a_i\mathbf{u}_i - \sum b_j\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{0} is a relation among the vectors of a basis of VV, so all bj=0b_j = 0.

□
Example 7.19 (Kernel and image of differentiation)¶

For D:ℙ4→ℙ3D\colon \mathbb{P}_4 \to \mathbb{P}_3, kerD\ker D is the set of constant polynomials (derivative 00), of dimension 11, and imD=ℙ3\operatorname{im} D = \mathbb{P}_3, since every a+bt+ct2a + bt + ct^2 is the derivative of at+b2t2+c3t3at + \frac{b}{2}t^2 + \frac{c}{3}t^3. Indeed 1+3=4=dimℙ41 + 3 = 4 = \dim \mathbb{P}_4. So DD is surjective but not injective, which is the reason an antiderivative is only determined ``up to a constant''.

Theorem 7.20 (A linear map is determined by its values on a basis)¶

Let (𝐯1,…,𝐯n)(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n) be a basis of VV, and let 𝐰1,…,𝐰n\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n be any vectors of WW. Remark 7.22 (Isomorphisms)¶

A bijective linear map is called an isomorphism. 7.4  The matrix of a linear map

💡 Intuition

Lecture 3 showed that a linear map ℝn→ℝm\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m is a matrix, by recording the images of the standard basis vectors as columns. An abstract space has no standard basis, but once we choose bases of VV and WW, coordinates turn vectors into columns of numbers and the same recipe produces a matrix. The matrix depends on the bases chosen; how it depends on them is the subject of the last section.

Definition 7.23 (Matrix of a linear map relative to bases)¶

Let f:V→Wf\colon V \to W be linear, let ℬ=(𝐯1,…,𝐯n)\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n) be a basis of VV and 𝒞=(𝐰1,…,𝐰m)\mathcal{C} = (\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_m) a basis of WW. 7.5  Change of basis and similar matrices

💡 Intuition

The same vector has different coordinates in different bases, and the same linear map has different matrices. A good choice of basis can make the matrix very simple: the reflection of Section 3 is diag(1,−1)\operatorname{diag}(1, -1) in a basis adapted to its line, but has fractions in the standard basis. This section shows how coordinates and matrices change when the basis changes. The answer is a single invertible matrix PP, and for a map from a space to itself the new matrix is P−1APP^{-1}AP. Matrices related in this way are called similar; they are different descriptions of one linear map.

Definition 7.30 (Change-of-basis matrix)¶

Let ℬ=(𝐛1,…,𝐛n)\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n) and 𝒞\mathcal{C} be two bases of the same space VV. The change-of-basis matrix from ℬ\mathcal{B} to 𝒞\mathcal{C} is the n×nn \times n matrix P𝒞←ℬ=([𝐛1]𝒞⋯[𝐛n]𝒞),P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = \begin{pmatrix} [\mathbf{b}_1]_{\mathcal{C}} & \cdots & [\mathbf{b}_n]_{\mathcal{C}} \end{pmatrix}, whose columns are the 𝒞\mathcal{C}-coordinates of the vectors of ℬ\mathcal{B}. It is the matrix [id]𝒞←ℬ[\operatorname{id}]_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} of the identity map id(𝐯)=𝐯\operatorname{id}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}.

Theorem 7.31 (Changing coordinates)¶

For every 𝐯∈V\mathbf{v} \in V, [𝐯]𝒞=P𝒞←ℬ[𝐯]ℬ[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{C}} = P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}\,[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}. The matrix P𝒞←ℬP_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} is invertible, and (P𝒞←ℬ)−1=Pℬ←𝒞(P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}})^{-1} = P_{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{C}}.

The first statement is the theorem on coordinates of the output for the identity map. By the composition theorem, Pℬ←𝒞P𝒞←ℬ=[id∘id]ℬ←ℬ=InP_{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{C}}\,P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = [\operatorname{id} \circ \operatorname{id}]_{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = I_n, since the identity map sends each 𝐛j\mathbf{b}_j to itself, with coordinate vector 𝐞j\mathbf{e}_j; likewise in the other order.

Remark 7.39 (Shared numbers do not guarantee similarity)¶

Remark 7.40 (Looking ahead)¶

The last example found a basis in which a matrix becomes diagonal, and the diagonal entries 22 and 33 were the roots of det(A−λI2)\det(A - \lambda I_2), a polynomial that by the theorem does not depend on the basis. Vectors such as 𝐛1\mathbf{b}_1 and 𝐛2\mathbf{b}_2, which a matrix only stretches, are eigenvectors, and the stretching factors are eigenvalues.
