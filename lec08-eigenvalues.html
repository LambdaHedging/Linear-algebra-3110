8.1  Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

💡 Intuition

A linear map can rotate, shear and stretch vectors in complicated ways, but often there are special directions that it does not turn at all: a vector in such a direction is only stretched, shrunk or flipped. These are the eigenvectors, and the stretching factors are the eigenvalues. Lecture 7 already met them: the reflection across a line keeps the vectors on the line and reverses those perpendicular to it, and the matrix (4−211)\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} stretches one direction by 22 and another by 33. In a basis of such directions the matrix of the map becomes diagonal, which is the goal of Section 3. This section sets up the definitions and proves the key structural fact: eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are always independent.

Definition 8.1 (Eigenvalue and eigenvector of a matrix)¶

Let AA be an n×nn \times n matrix. A real number λ\lambda is an eigenvalue of AA if there is a nonzero vector 𝐱∈ℝn\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n with A𝐱=λ𝐱.A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} . Such an 𝐱\mathbf{x} is called an eigenvector of AA belonging to (or corresponding to) λ\lambda.

Remark 8.2 (Two points in the definition)¶

An eigenvector must be nonzero: A0=λ0A\mathbf{0} = \lambda\mathbf{0} holds for every λ\lambda, so allowing 𝐱=0\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} would make every number an eigenvalue. An eigenvalue, on the other hand, may be 00: λ=0\lambda = 0 is an eigenvalue exactly when A𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} has a nonzero solution. Also, only square matrices have eigenvalues, since A𝐱A\mathbf{x} and λ𝐱\lambda\mathbf{x} must lie in the same space. In this course the scalars are real numbers, so eigenvalues are real by definition; complex eigenvalues are discussed briefly in Section 2.

Definition 8.4 (Eigenspace)¶

Let λ\lambda be an eigenvalue of the n×nn \times n matrix AA. The eigenspace of AA for λ\lambda is Eλ={𝐱∈ℝn:A𝐱=λ𝐱}=Nul(A−λIn),E_\lambda = \{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} \,\} = \operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda I_n) , the set of all eigenvectors belonging to λ\lambda together with the zero vector.

Proposition 8.5 (Eigenvalues through the null space)¶

Let AA be an n×nn \times n matrix and λ\lambda a number. Then A𝐱=λ𝐱A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} if and only if (A−λIn)𝐱=0(A - \lambda I_n)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}. Consequently:

  1. λ\lambda is an eigenvalue of AA if and only if Nul(A−λIn)≠{0}\operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda I_n) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\};
  2. every eigenspace EλE_\lambda is a subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, and a basis of it is found by solving (A−λIn)𝐱=0(A - \lambda I_n)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0};
  3. 00 is an eigenvalue of AA if and only if AA is not invertible.

Since λ𝐱=λIn𝐱\lambda\mathbf{x} = \lambda I_n\mathbf{x}, the equation A𝐱=λ𝐱A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} is the same as A𝐱−λIn𝐱=0A\mathbf{x} - \lambda I_n\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}, that is, (A−λIn)𝐱=0(A - \lambda I_n)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}, by the distributive law of Lecture 4. Statement (1) is now the definition. For (2), EλE_\lambda is a null space, hence a subspace by Lecture 6, and Lecture 7 gives a basis. For (3), take λ=0\lambda = 0: 00 is an eigenvalue if and only if Nul(A)≠{0}\operatorname{Nul}(A) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}, which by the invertibility theorem means that AA is not invertible.

Definition 8.8 (Eigenvalue and eigenvector of a linear map)¶

Let VV be a vector space and f:V→Vf\colon V \to V a linear map. A number λ\lambda is an eigenvalue of ff if f(𝐯)=λ𝐯f(\mathbf{v}) = \lambda\mathbf{v} for some nonzero 𝐯∈V\mathbf{v} \in V, and such a 𝐯\mathbf{v} is an eigenvector of ff belonging to λ\lambda. The eigenspace Eλ={𝐯∈V:f(𝐯)=λ𝐯}E_\lambda = \{\mathbf{v} \in V : f(\mathbf{v}) = \lambda\mathbf{v}\} is the kernel of the linear map 𝐯↦f(𝐯)−λ𝐯\mathbf{v} \mapsto f(\mathbf{v}) - \lambda\mathbf{v}, hence a subspace of VV. For f(𝐱)=A𝐱f(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} on ℝn\mathbb{R}^n these are the eigenvalues, eigenvectors and eigenspaces of AA.

Remark 8.10 (Exponential functions)¶

On the space of functions ℝ→ℝ\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} that can be differentiated any number of times (a subspace of the function space of Lecture 6), differentiation is linear, and ddteat=aeat\frac{d}{dt}e^{at} = a\,e^{at}: every real number aa is an eigenvalue, with eigenvector eate^{at}. This is why exponentials appear whenever one solves linear differential equations. The space here is not finite-dimensional, so it lies outside the theory of this lecture, but the definition applies unchanged.

Theorem 8.11 (Eigenvalues of a map and of its matrix)¶

Let f:V→Vf\colon V \to V be linear, let ℬ\mathcal{B} be a basis of VV, and let A=[f]ℬA = [f]_{\mathcal{B}}, the matrix of ff under the basis ℬ\mathcal{B}. Hence ff and AA have the same eigenvalues, and 𝐯\mathbf{v} is an eigenvector of ff if and only if its coordinate vector [𝐯]ℬ[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} is an eigenvector of AA, for the same eigenvalue.

By Lecture 7, [f(𝐯)]ℬ=A[𝐯]ℬ[f(\mathbf{v})]_{\mathcal{B}} = A\,[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}, and by Lecture 6, [λ𝐯]ℬ=λ[𝐯]ℬ[\lambda\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \lambda\,[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}. Finally, 𝐯≠0\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0} if and only if [𝐯]ℬ≠0[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} \neq \mathbf{0}.

Theorem 8.14 (Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent)¶

Let f:V→Vf\colon V \to V be linear (in particular f(𝐱)=A𝐱f(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} on ℝn\mathbb{R}^n), and let 𝐯1,…,𝐯p\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p be eigenvectors of ff belonging to distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λp\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p. Then 𝐯1,…,𝐯p\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p are linearly independent.

Corollary 8.15 (At most nn eigenvalues)¶

An n×nn \times n matrix, or a linear map on an nn-dimensional space, has at most nn distinct eigenvalues.

One eigenvector for each distinct eigenvalue gives an independent list, by the theorem, and an independent list in an nn-dimensional space has at most nn vectors, by Lecture 6.

8.2  The characteristic equation

💡 Intuition

Section 1 could test a given number λ\lambda, but how do we find the eigenvalues in the first place? The test was whether A−λIA - \lambda I has a nonzero null space, that is, whether A−λIA - \lambda I fails to be invertible, and Lecture 5 turned invertibility into a single number, the determinant. So the eigenvalues are the roots of the equation det(A−λI)=0\det(A - \lambda I) = 0, whose left side is a polynomial in λ\lambda of degree nn. Finding eigenvalues becomes finding roots of a polynomial; finding eigenvectors is then row reduction, as before.

Theorem 8.16 (Eigenvalues are the roots of a determinant)¶

A number λ\lambda is an eigenvalue of the n×nn \times n matrix AA if and only if det(A−λIn)=0.\det(A - \lambda I_n) = 0 .

By the proposition of Section 1, λ\lambda is an eigenvalue if and only if Nul(A−λIn)≠{0}\operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda I_n) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}, if and only if A−λInA - \lambda I_n is not invertible, by the invertibility theorem, if and only if det(A−λIn)=0\det(A - \lambda I_n) = 0, by Lecture 5.

□
Definition 8.17 (Characteristic polynomial and equation)¶

The characteristic polynomial of an n×nn \times n matrix AA is pA(λ)=det(A−λIn)p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n), and det(A−λIn)=0\det(A - \lambda I_n) = 0 is its characteristic equation. Here λ\lambda is a variable: the determinant of A−λInA - \lambda I_n is computed by the expansion formula of Lecture 5, which uses only sums and products of the entries and so makes sense when the entries are polynomials in λ\lambda. Substituting a number for λ\lambda gives the determinant of the corresponding numerical matrix.

Example 8.18 (The 2×22 \times 2 case)¶

For A=(abcd)A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, pA(λ)=det(a−λbcd−λ)=(a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=λ2−(a+d)λ+(ad−bc).\begin{aligned} p_A(\lambda) &= \det\begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc \\ &= \lambda^2 - (a + d)\,\lambda + (ad - bc) . \end{aligned} The coefficient of λ\lambda is minus the trace trA=a+d\operatorname{tr} A = a + d, the sum of the diagonal entries, and the constant term is detA\det A. Remark 8.19 (The degree of pAp_A)¶

The characteristic polynomial of an n×nn \times n matrix has degree nn. Indeed, expanding det(A−λIn)\det(A - \lambda I_n) completely, as was done for 3×33 \times 3 matrices in Lecture 5, gives a sum of n!n! terms, each a product of nn entries taken from different rows and different columns. Only the diagonal entries aii−λa_{ii} - \lambda contain λ\lambda, and only one term, the product (a11−λ)(a22−λ)⋯(ann−λ)(a_{11} - \lambda)(a_{22} - \lambda)\cdots(a_{nn} - \lambda) of all of them, has degree nn; every other term leaves out at least two diagonal entries and has degree at most n−2n - 2. Hence pA(λ)=(−1)nλn+(−1)n−1(trA)λn−1+⋯+detA,p_A(\lambda) = (-1)^{n}\lambda^{n} + (-1)^{n-1}(\operatorname{tr} A)\,\lambda^{n-1} + \cdots + \det A , where trA=a11+⋯+ann\operatorname{tr} A = a_{11} + \cdots + a_{nn} is the trace of AA (the terms of degree n−1n - 1 also come only from the diagonal product). The constant term is detA\det A, by setting λ=0\lambda = 0: pA(0)=det(A−0In)=detAp_A(0) = \det(A - 0\,I_n) = \det A.

Definition 8.20 (Multiplicity of an eigenvalue)¶

Let λ0\lambda_0 be an eigenvalue of AA. Its multiplicity (also called algebraic multiplicity) is the largest integer mm such that (λ−λ0)m(\lambda - \lambda_0)^{m} divides pA(λ)p_A(\lambda). The dimension of Eλ0E_{\lambda_0} is sometimes called the geometric multiplicity of λ0\lambda_0.

Corollary 8.21 (Counting eigenvalues)¶

An n×nn \times n matrix has at most nn eigenvalues, even when each is counted as many times as its multiplicity. If pAp_A is a product of linear factors, pA(λ)=(λ1−λ)(λ2−λ)⋯(λn−λ),p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda_1 - \lambda)(\lambda_2 - \lambda)\cdots(\lambda_n - \lambda), with the eigenvalues listed according to multiplicity, then λ1+λ2+⋯+λn=trA,λ1λ2⋯λn=detA.\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots + \lambda_n = \operatorname{tr} A, \qquad \lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n = \det A .

The multiplicities of the distinct roots of a polynomial of degree nn add up to at most nn. If pAp_A is the displayed product, multiplying out shows that the coefficient of λn−1\lambda^{n-1} is (−1)n−1(λ1+⋯+λn)(-1)^{n-1}(\lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n) and the constant term is λ1⋯λn\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n; compare with the remark on the degree of pAp_A.

Theorem 8.22 (Triangular matrices)¶

The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries, and the multiplicity of an eigenvalue is the number of times it occurs on the diagonal.

Proposition 8.23 (Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues)¶

If B=P−1APB = P^{-1}AP, then pA(λ)=pB(λ)p_A(\lambda) = p_B(\lambda), so AA and BB have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities. Moreover B𝐱=λ𝐱B\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} if and only if A(P𝐱)=λ(P𝐱)A(P\mathbf{x}) = \lambda\,(P\mathbf{x}), so 𝐱↦P𝐱\mathbf{x} \mapsto P\mathbf{x} carries each eigenspace of BB onto the eigenspace of AA for the same eigenvalue, and the two eigenspaces have the same dimension.

By part (3) of Lecture 7's theorem, pA(λ)=pB(λ)p_A(\lambda) = p_B(\lambda) for every number λ\lambda; two polynomials that agree at infinitely many numbers are equal (their difference has infinitely many roots), so pA(λ)=pB(λ)p_A(\lambda) = p_B(\lambda) as polynomials. Next, B𝐱=λ𝐱B\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} means P−1AP𝐱=λ𝐱P^{-1}AP\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}; multiplying by PP on the left gives A(P𝐱)=λP𝐱A(P\mathbf{x}) = \lambda P\mathbf{x}, and multiplying that by P−1P^{-1} returns the first equation. Since PP is invertible, the map 𝐱↦P𝐱\mathbf{x} \mapsto P\mathbf{x} is a bijective linear map, so it carries a basis of the eigenspace of BB to a basis of the eigenspace of AA.

Remark 8.24 (The procedure, and a warning)¶

To find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of AA: (1) compute pA(λ)=det(A−λIn)p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n), using row and column operations or cofactor expansions as in Lecture 5, and try to keep it factored; (2) find its real roots, the eigenvalues; (3) for each eigenvalue λ\lambda, solve (A−λIn)𝐱=0(A - \lambda I_n)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} by row reduction; the parametric vector form gives a basis of EλE_\lambda.

8.3  Diagonalization

💡 Intuition

A diagonal matrix is the simplest kind of matrix: it stretches each coordinate axis by its own factor, and its powers are computed entry by entry. A matrix AA is diagonalizable if, after a change of basis, it becomes diagonal; by Lecture 7 this means A=PDP−1A = PDP^{-1}. The columns of PP then have to be eigenvectors, so the question is whether there are enough independent eigenvectors to make a basis. The answer is a counting criterion: add up the dimensions of the eigenspaces and compare with nn. Two things can go wrong, and the examples at the end show both: the characteristic polynomial may have too few real roots, and an eigenspace may be smaller than the multiplicity of its eigenvalue.

Definition 8.29 (Diagonalizable)¶

An n×nn \times n matrix AA is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix, that is, if A=PDP−1A = PDP^{-1}, or equivalently P−1AP=DP^{-1}AP = D, for some invertible matrix PP and some diagonal matrix DD. A linear map f:V→Vf\colon V \to V is diagonalizable if VV has a basis ℬ\mathcal{B} for which [f]ℬ[f]_{\mathcal{B}} is diagonal.

Theorem 8.30 (The diagonalization theorem)¶

An n×nn \times n matrix AA is diagonalizable if and only if AA has nn linearly independent eigenvectors, that is, if and only if ℝn\mathbb{R}^n has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of AA. More precisely, for an invertible P=(𝐯1⋯𝐯n)P = (\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n) and a diagonal D=diag(λ1,…,λn)D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n), A=PDP−1⇔A𝐯j=λj𝐯j for j=1,…,n.A = PDP^{-1} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\mathbf{v}_j = \lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j \ \text{ for } j = 1, \dots, n . So the columns of PP are eigenvectors, and the diagonal entries of DD are the corresponding eigenvalues, in the same order.

A=PDP−1A = PDP^{-1} is equivalent to AP=PDAP = PD (multiply on the right by PP, or by P−1P^{-1}). Compare the two sides column by column. Write D=(λ1𝐞1λ2𝐞2⋯λn𝐞n),D = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1\mathbf{e}_1 & \lambda_2\mathbf{e}_2 & \cdots & \lambda_n\mathbf{e}_n \end{pmatrix}, where 𝐞1,…,𝐞n\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n are the standard unit vectors of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, so that column jj of DD is λj𝐞j\lambda_j\mathbf{e}_j. Column jj of APAP is A𝐯jA\mathbf{v}_j, and column jj of PDPD is PP times column jj of DD, that is, P(λj𝐞j)=λjP𝐞j=λj𝐯jP(\lambda_j\mathbf{e}_j) = \lambda_j P\mathbf{e}_j = \lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j. So AP=PDAP = PD if and only if A𝐯j=λj𝐯jA\mathbf{v}_j = \lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j for all jj. If AA is diagonalizable, the columns of the invertible matrix PP are therefore nn eigenvectors, and they are independent by the invertibility theorem. Conversely, if 𝐯1,…,𝐯n\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n are independent eigenvectors, the matrix PP with these columns is invertible by the same theorem, and A=PDP−1A = PDP^{-1}.

□
Remark 8.31 (The meaning of PP and DD)¶

In the language of Lecture 7: if ℬ=(𝐯1,…,𝐯n)\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n) is a basis of eigenvectors and f(𝐱)=A𝐱f(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}, then D=[f]ℬD = [f]_{\mathcal{B}} is the matrix of the same map in the eigenvector basis, and P=Pℰ←ℬP = P_{\mathcal{E} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}. In coordinates relative to ℬ\mathcal{B} the map just multiplies the jj-th coordinate by λj\lambda_j. Likewise, a linear map f:V→Vf\colon V \to V is diagonalizable exactly when VV has a basis of eigenvectors of ff, and then [f]ℬ=diag(λ1,…,λn)[f]_{\mathcal{B}} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n); by the theorem of Section 1 and Lecture 7, this happens if and only if one (equivalently, every) matrix of ff is diagonalizable.

Corollary 8.32 (Distinct eigenvalues suffice)¶

An n×nn \times n matrix with nn distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

One eigenvector for each eigenvalue gives nn eigenvectors, which are independent by the theorem of Section 1; apply the diagonalization theorem.

□
Remark 8.33 (The condition is not necessary)¶

The identity InI_n has the single eigenvalue 11, of multiplicity nn, and it is diagonal. Repeated eigenvalues do not prevent diagonalization; what matters is the size of the eigenspaces, as the next two results show.

Lemma 8.34 (Bases of eigenspaces combine)¶

Let λ1,…,λp\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p be distinct eigenvalues of AA (or of a linear map f:V→Vf\colon V \to V), and choose a basis of each eigenspace EλiE_{\lambda_i}. Then all these vectors together form a linearly independent list.

Suppose a linear combination of the vectors in the bases of the eigenspaces, taken all together, is equal to 0\mathbf{0}, and let 𝐰i\mathbf{w}_i be the part of it that uses the basis vectors of EλiE_{\lambda_i}. Then 𝐰i∈Eλi\mathbf{w}_i \in E_{\lambda_i} and 𝐰1+⋯+𝐰p=0\mathbf{w}_1 + \cdots + \mathbf{w}_p = \mathbf{0}. If some 𝐰i\mathbf{w}_i were nonzero, the nonzero ones would be eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues with sum 0\mathbf{0}, a nontrivial relation among them, contradicting the theorem of Section 1. So every 𝐰i=0\mathbf{w}_i = \mathbf{0}, and since the vectors used in 𝐰i\mathbf{w}_i form a basis of EλiE_{\lambda_i}, all their coefficients are 00.

□
Theorem 8.35 (Eigenspace dimension is at most the multiplicity)¶

If λ0\lambda_0 is an eigenvalue of AA with multiplicity mm, then 1≤dimEλ0≤m1 \le \dim E_{\lambda_0} \le m.

Eλ0E_{\lambda_0} contains an eigenvector, so its dimension is at least 11. Suppose A−λ0InA - \lambda_0 I_n has rank kk; then dimEλ0=dimNul(A−λ0In)=n−k\dim E_{\lambda_0} = \dim \operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda_0 I_n) = n - k by the rank theorem, and we must show n−k≤mn - k \le m. Row reduce A−λ0InA - \lambda_0 I_n to an echelon form: by Lecture 4 there is an invertible matrix BB, a product of elementary matrices, such that B(A−λ0In)B(A - \lambda_0 I_n) is in echelon form. It has kk pivots, so its last n−kn - k rows are zero.

Now consider B(A−λIn)B(A - \lambda I_n), whose entries are polynomials in λ\lambda of degree at most 11. Substituting λ=λ0\lambda = \lambda_0 gives B(A−λ0In)B(A - \lambda_0 I_n), so every entry in the last n−kn - k rows of B(A−λIn)B(A - \lambda I_n) vanishes at λ=λ0\lambda = \lambda_0. We use one fact about polynomials: λ−a\lambda - a divides a polynomial f(λ)f(\lambda) if and only if f(a)=0f(a) = 0. Hence every entry of these n−kn - k rows is divisible by λ−λ0\lambda - \lambda_0. So (λ−λ0)n−k(\lambda - \lambda_0)^{n-k} divides pA(λ)p_A(\lambda), and n−k≤mn - k \le m by the definition of multiplicity.

Theorem 8.36 (The criterion for diagonalization)¶

Let AA be an n×nn \times n matrix with distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λp\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p, of multiplicities m1,…,mpm_1, \dots, m_p. The following are equivalent.

  1. AA is diagonalizable.
  2. The dimensions of the eigenspaces add up to nn: dimEλ1+dimEλ2+⋯+dimEλp=n.\dim E_{\lambda_1} + \dim E_{\lambda_2} + \cdots + \dim E_{\lambda_p} = n .
  3. pAp_A is a product of real linear factors, pA(λ)=(λ1−λ)m1⋯(λp−λ)mp,p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda_1 - \lambda)^{m_1}\cdots(\lambda_p - \lambda)^{m_p}, and dimEλi=mi\dim E_{\lambda_i} = m_i for every ii.
In that case, putting together bases of the eigenspaces gives nn independent eigenvectors, which are the columns of PP in the diagonalization theorem.

Write di=dimEλid_i = \dim E_{\lambda_i}.

(1) ⇒\Rightarrow (2). By the diagonalization theorem there is a basis of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n consisting of eigenvectors. Each of them lies in some EλiE_{\lambda_i}, and those lying in EλiE_{\lambda_i} are independent, so there are at most did_i of them (Lecture 6). Hence n≤d1+⋯+dpn \le d_1 + \cdots + d_p. On the other hand, the combined bases of the eigenspaces form an independent list of d1+⋯+dpd_1 + \cdots + d_p vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, by the lemma, so d1+⋯+dp≤nd_1 + \cdots + d_p \le n.

(2) ⇒\Rightarrow (1). The combined bases give nn independent eigenvectors, by the lemma; apply the diagonalization theorem.

(2) ⇔\Leftrightarrow (3). By the definition of multiplicity, pA(λ)=(λ−λ1)m1⋯(λ−λp)mpq(λ)p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda_1)^{m_1}\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_p)^{m_p}\, q(\lambda) for a polynomial qq with q(λi)≠0q(\lambda_i) \neq 0 for all ii; since every real root of pAp_A is one of the λi\lambda_i, qq has no real roots, and comparing degrees, m1+⋯+mp+degq=nm_1 + \cdots + m_p + \deg q = n. Using the theorem, d1+⋯+dp≤m1+⋯+mp≤n.d_1 + \cdots + d_p \;\le\; m_1 + \cdots + m_p \;\le\; n . So d1+⋯+dp=nd_1 + \cdots + d_p = n if and only if both inequalities are equalities, that is, di=mid_i = m_i for every ii (each di≤mid_i \le m_i) and degq=0\deg q = 0; in the latter case qq is the constant (−1)n(-1)^{n}, the leading coefficient of pAp_A (the remark on the degree), which is statement (3).

□
Remark 8.37 (How to diagonalize)¶

(1) Find the eigenvalues from pAp_A. If pAp_A has fewer than nn real roots counted with multiplicity, stop: AA is not diagonalizable. (2) For each eigenvalue find a basis of EλE_\lambda. An eigenvalue of multiplicity 11 always has dimEλ=1\dim E_\lambda = 1, by the theorem, so only repeated eigenvalues need checking. (3) If dimEλ\dim E_\lambda equals the multiplicity for every λ\lambda, let PP have the basis vectors as columns and DD the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal. (4) Check AP=PDAP = PD, which avoids computing P−1P^{-1}.

Example 8.41 (Non-example: a rotation)¶

The rotation A=(01−10)A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} is not diagonalizable: it has no eigenvalues at all (Section 2), let alone two independent eigenvectors. (Over the complex numbers it is diagonalizable, with D=diag(i,−i)D = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i); see the remark.)

Proposition 8.42 (Triangular with a constant diagonal)¶

Let AA be an n×nn \times n triangular matrix whose diagonal entries are all equal to cc. Then AA is diagonalizable if and only if A=cInA = cI_n.

By the theorem on triangular matrices, cc is the only eigenvalue of AA. If A=PDP−1A = PDP^{-1} with DD diagonal, the diagonal entries of DD are eigenvalues of AA, by the diagonalization theorem, so D=cInD = cI_n and A=P(cIn)P−1=cPP−1=cInA = P(cI_n)P^{-1} = c\,PP^{-1} = cI_n. Conversely, cIncI_n is diagonal.

Remark 8.46 (Looking ahead)¶

Diagonalization turns powers AkA^{k} into powers of numbers, which is the key to linear recurrences and to systems that evolve step by step, 𝐱k+1=A𝐱k\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k: in an eigenvector basis each coordinate is simply multiplied by its eigenvalue at every step. The two obstacles met in this section, eigenvalues that are not real and eigenspaces that are too small, never occur for symmetric matrices (AT=AA^{T} = A); proving this requires the inner product, which is still to come.
